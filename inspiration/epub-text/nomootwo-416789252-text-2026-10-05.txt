Chapter One: The Girl Who Was Never Told No

Draconia Malfoy was eleven years old, and in all eleven of those years, nobody had ever told her no.

Not the house-elves, who weren't allowed to. Not the tutors her father hired every autumn and dismissed every spring. Not the white peacocks on the south lawn, who scattered whenever she walked through them, which she had long ago decided was a form of respect.

Not even her mother, who said "we'll see, darling" instead. In Malfoy Manor, "we'll see" meant yes. It just took longer.

So on the morning they went to Diagon Alley, when Narcissa drew the silver brush through her daughter's hair for the hundredth stroke and said, "You will be kind to the other girls at school, won't you?", Draconia took it as a suggestion.

"I'm always kind," she said. "To people who deserve it."

In the mirror, her mother's mouth curved. "And who decides who deserves it?"

"I do. Obviously."

"She has you there, Cissy."

Her father stood in the doorway, already cloaked, silver-headed cane under one arm. Lucius Malfoy never seemed to walk into rooms. He simply appeared in them, as if the room had been waiting for him and was relieved he'd finally come.

He tipped her chin up with one gloved finger and studied her face the way he studied the family silver: checking for tarnish.

"You'll meet a great many people this year," he said. "Most of them won't matter. Remember one thing, Draconia. A Malfoy never asks for anything. A Malfoy is offered things. Remember that, and you will never once be disappointed."

She remembered it.

It would be years before she understood he'd been wrong.

✦ ✦ ✦

Ollivander's shop smelled of dust and old varnish. The old man had pale eyes like two moons that didn't blink nearly often enough.

The first wand he handed her shattered a vase. The second set the counter lamp on fire. The third did nothing at all, which she found more insulting than the other two combined.

The fourth was pale and slightly knotted. The moment her fingers closed around it, something warm ran up her arm like a secret, and silver sparks drifted to the ceiling and hung there, glittering.

"Hawthorn," Ollivander murmured. "Ten inches. Unicorn hair."

"Is that good?" She'd meant it to sound bored. It came out curious.

"Hawthorn is a strange wood, Miss Malfoy. It heals, and it curses, and it's never quite sure which it prefers." He looked at her for a long moment. "It tends to choose owners who are at war with themselves."

"I'm not at war with anything."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

She decided she did not like him.

✦ ✦ ✦

Her mother had gone to see about an owl, and her father had business on a street he preferred she didn't know the name of. So Draconia stood alone on the fitting stool in Madam Malkin's while a witch in lilac pinned her very first Hogwarts robe, and she was bored enough to die.

The bell over the door jingled.

The boy who came in was small and thin, and his clothes looked like they'd been made for someone much bigger who had since lost interest in them. His black hair stuck up at the back as if it had lost an argument with a pillow. His round glasses were held together at the bridge with tape.

Draconia had never seen anyone fix their glasses with tape. She hadn't known it was allowed.

Madam Malkin put him on the stool beside hers.

"Hogwarts?" said Draconia.

He jumped. "Er. Yes."

"Me too. I'm going to be in Slytherin. Everyone in my family has been, going back forever. Which house were your people in?"

"My people?"

"Your parents. Slytherin? Ravenclaw at least?" She looked him up and down. "Hufflepuff?"

Something in his face closed, very quietly, like a door shutting in another room. "I don't know."

"You don't know?"

"No."

People always knew. Knowing was the entire point of having a family. She decided his must be nobody, and felt a small, cool flicker of satisfaction at having worked it out.

Through the window she could see an enormous man waiting outside: wild beard, coat like a haystack, two ice creams in hands the size of dustbin lids.

"Goodness," she said. "Is that with you?"

"That's Hagrid. He works at Hogwarts."

"Oh, I've heard of him. He's a sort of servant, isn't he? Father says he lives in a hut and sets fire to things. Father says they should never have..."

"He's the best person I've ever met."

He didn't say it loudly. That was the odd part. He said it the way you'd say the sky was up, and then he looked straight at her through those ridiculous taped glasses.

His eyes were very green. Startlingly green. Like the Slytherin banners, but alive.

Irrelevant, Draconia informed herself. Completely irrelevant.

For the first time in her life, she had been corrected. Her cheeks went hot. She lifted her chin.

"You're staring, Four Eyes. It's rude."

"You were insulting my friend," said the boy. "That's ruder."

Madam Malkin said "All done, dear!" with the brightness of a woman who wanted this conversation over, and Draconia hopped down.

"Well," she said, gathering her dignity like a cloak. "I expect I'll see you at school."

"I expect so," said the boy, and went back to looking at the robes.

She was halfway down the street, walking faster than a Malfoy should, before she realized she'd never asked his name.

It was ten more steps before she realized he hadn't asked hers.

That was the part that stayed with her.

✦ ✦ ✦

Vivienne Crabbe and Greta Goyle had been assigned to her the way the peacocks had. They came with the house. Their fathers did whatever Lucius Malfoy said, so Vivienne and Greta did whatever his daughter said. They carried her trunk onto the Hogwarts Express and stood outside her compartment like a pair of stone gargoyles guarding a gate.

It was Pansy Parkinson who brought the news, flushed and breathless, hanging off the door frame.

"He's on the train! Harry Potter! Third carriage from the back. He's got the scar and everything!"

Draconia set down her book very slowly.

Harry Potter. The Boy Who Lived. The baby who had survived the Dark Lord when nobody else ever had. Harry Potter was famous. Harry Potter was important. And important people, her father always said, belonged with other important people.

It was obvious where he ought to be sitting.

"Vivienne. Greta." She stood and smoothed her new robes. "We're going for a walk."

✦ ✦ ✦

The compartment had its door half open and a snowdrift of sweet wrappers on the floor. There was a freckled boy with bright red hair and a smudge of dirt on his nose. And next to him...

The taped glasses. The hair that had lost the argument. And now, because he'd pushed his fringe back to eat a pumpkin pasty, a thin scar shaped like a bolt of lightning.

Draconia stopped so suddenly that Greta walked into the back of her.

Oh, she thought. Oh, no.

Then, because she was a Malfoy, she rearranged her face into something cool and pleasant and stepped inside as if she'd meant to all along.

"So it's true. Harry Potter."

He looked up, and she watched him recognize her.

"You're the girl from the robe shop."

"Draconia Malfoy." She waited for the name to land. On the red-haired boy, it did; he made a noise like a snort being swallowed. On Harry, nothing. He'd never heard of her. He'd never heard of any of them.

She pressed on. "Some families here are worth knowing and some really aren't. It's not your fault you can't tell yet. You weren't raised with us." Her gaze drifted, very deliberately, to the red-haired boy's lumpy, hand-knitted jumper. "Red hair, secondhand robes, sandwiches from home. You must be a Weasley."

The Weasley boy's ears went the color of his hair.

"We have a compartment at the front," Draconia said. "It's quieter. The company's better. You may sit with us."

And she held out her hand.

Not sideways, the way boys did it for shaking. She held it the way the ladies did at her mother's summer parties: palm down, fingers softly curled, wrist lifted, waiting to be taken. Every boy she had ever met had known exactly what to do with it.

Harry Potter looked at her hand.

Then at Ron Weasley's furious red ears.

Then back at her.

"I think I can work out which company's better on my own," he said. "Thanks."

The compartment went very quiet.

Her hand stayed in the air between them. One second. Two. Behind her she could feel Vivienne peering at it, trying to work out whether she was supposed to do something about it.

She took it back.

"You'll regret that, Four Eyes."

"It's Harry."

"I know what your name is."

He frowned at her then. Properly frowned. His dark brows pulled together and a small, stubborn crease appeared right between them, and his green eyes went sharp and fixed on her face as if nothing else in the carriage existed.

Nobody had ever looked at her like that. People looked up at her, or away from her, or past her toward her father.

He was looking at her.

Do that again, thought some small, treacherous part of her.

She turned on her heel so sharply that her hair whipped Greta in the face, and walked out with her back very straight, and did not look behind her. Not once. Not even when Ron Weasley burst out laughing.

✦ ✦ ✦

She made it two carriages before she had to stop.

She stood at the corridor window, watching the hills slide by in late gold light, holding her right hand very tightly in her left because it was shaking and she didn't want anyone to see.

It was such a small thing. A boy with taped glasses had said no thanks. That was all.

But it felt like walking down a staircase in the dark and finding one more step that wasn't there.

"Want a Chocolate Frog?" said Vivienne, after a long, uncertain pause.

She took it without looking. She opened the box. The frog took one look at her, sprang onto the window, and leapt through the gap at the top, straight out into the passing countryside.

Draconia stared after it.

"Even the frog," she said, to nobody at all.

Fine, she thought. Fine. Let him laugh with his Weasley.

One day, Harry Potter would come to her. He'd want something, and he'd ask for it, and she would make him ask properly.

And then, perhaps, she would say yes.

In the meantime, she thought, remembering that small stubborn crease between his brows, she would simply have to make him frown at her again.

As often as possible.

For revenge. Obviously.

✦ ✦ ✦

Hogsmeade station was dark, cold and chaotic. First-years spilled out of the train in a shivering crush, and the enormous man from the robe-shop window was bellowing for them over the noise, holding a lantern high.

The step down from the carriage was steep and slick with rain, and somebody behind her shoved.

Draconia missed it.

For one lurching second there was nothing under her foot, the same drop she'd felt in the corridor, only real this time. Then a hand closed around her wrist and held on.

She found her balance on the platform with her heart going like a trapped bird. She looked up.

Taped glasses. Rain on the lenses. Green eyes, very close.

"You all right?" said Harry Potter.

For a moment, just one, she forgot every single word she knew.

Then she remembered some.

"Unhand me," said Draconia Malfoy.

He let go at once, as if she'd burned him, and rolled his eyes, and disappeared into the crowd after Ron Weasley before she could think of anything more cutting to add.

Her wrist was warm where his hand had been.

Horrible, she thought, cradling it. Absolutely horrible. I'll probably have to have it disinfected.

She did not rub her thumb over the spot the whole way across the lake. She was simply cold.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Great Hall was more beautiful than anything at the Manor, which she found annoying. A thousand candles floated in midair, and the ceiling had become the night sky, soft with cloud and scattered with stars. Without meaning to, she found the long, winding constellation she was named for, and felt oddly steadied by it.

"Malfoy, Draconia!"

She walked to the stool like it was a throne.

The Sorting Hat barely brushed her hair.

"SLYTHERIN!"

The table on the far left erupted. She took her seat to applause that was, she felt, exactly the right volume, and did not once look for a pair of taped glasses among the first-years still waiting.

She only happened to be facing that way when they called "Potter, Harry."

The whole Hall went still. She watched him walk to the stool, small and thin and trying very hard not to look at anyone, and watched the Hat slide down over his eyes.

It took a long time.

It took so long that the whispers stopped. So long that she realized she was holding her breath, and was furious with herself, and let it out, and caught herself holding it again.

Slytherin, she thought, before she could stop it. Say Slytherin.

"GRYFFINDOR!"

The far right table exploded. A pair of red-haired twins were chanting and banging their goblets. Harry pulled the Hat off, and his face was so openly relieved that it hurt to look at.

Draconia looked at her plate instead.

It didn't matter. Obviously it didn't matter. She'd only been curious.

But just before he sat down among the Gryffindors, she saw him glance across the Hall. Past the Ravenclaws. Past the Hufflepuffs. All the way to the Slytherin table.

To her.

Just for a second.

Then Ron Weasley was thumping him on the back, and he was gone into a sea of red and gold.

✦ ✦ ✦

That night, under green silk bed hangings, beneath a lake whose black water pressed against the dormitory windows, Pansy Parkinson would not stop talking.

"...and his scar is actually shaped like lightning, and Millicent says he grew up with Muggles, can you imagine, and did you see him look over here? Do you think he was looking at me?"

"No," said Draconia, and drew her curtains shut.

In the green dark, with her new hawthorn wand under her pillow and her wrist still, stupidly, remembering, she tried saying his name.

Not aloud. Just in her head, to see how it felt.

Harry.

It felt like losing. She didn't like it one bit.

"Four Eyes," she whispered to the dark instead, which was safer.

Then she fell asleep planning exactly how to make him frown at her tomorrow.

She would spend six more years at it.

✦ ✦ ✦

End of Chapter One

Chapter 1.1: The Empty Seat

Year One. The first week of term.

On her first morning at Hogwarts, Draconia came down to breakfast early, took the place at the end of the Slytherin table nearest the doors, and put her schoolbag on the bench beside her.

"Is someone sitting there?" said Pansy Parkinson.

"No."

"Then can I..."

"No."

Pansy sat down opposite instead and looked at the bag as if it might explain itself. It was a very good bag, black dragonhide with a silver clasp, and it explained nothing.

Draconia buttered a slice of toast. She had chosen this end of the table for the light, which came down from the high windows and was kinder to her hair. That it also gave her a clear view across the Hall to the Gryffindors was an accident of architecture.

She had worked it all out last night under the green bed hangings. A Malfoy never asks, her father had said. A Malfoy is offered things. So she could not walk over there and tell Harry Potter to come and sit with her. That would be asking, and she had done quite enough of that on the train.

But nobody had ever said a word against leaving a space.

He would see it. He'd had a whole night in a tower full of Weasleys by now, which ought to bring anyone to their senses. He would look across the Hall and notice that the best seat at the best table was being kept, and he would understand who it was being kept for, and he would come over and ask if it was taken.

And she would think about it.

It was really very generous of her.

Vivienne and Greta had not asked about the bag at all. They never asked about anything. To see what would happen, Draconia said, "The porridge here is better than at home," which was a lie so large that it ought to have fallen off the table.

"Yes," said Vivienne.

Greta nodded, and went on eating it.

Pansy made a face at her own bowl. "It's glue," she said. "Are you feeling all right?"

Draconia looked at her for a moment longer than she meant to.

He came in at ten past eight with Ron Weasley talking at his elbow. His hair had got worse overnight. He didn't look at the Slytherin table once. He sat down with his back to it and started filling his plate, sausages and eggs and fried bread, quickly, with one eye on the dish as though somebody might take it away.

Draconia had never seen anybody eat like that. At the Manor, food simply kept arriving.

"Why are you watching Potter?" said Pansy.

"I'm watching the door."

"Why are you watching the door?"

"Eat something, Pansy."

✦ ✦ ✦

She hadn't planned to speak to him. But at five to nine she was crossing the entrance hall with Vivienne and Greta when he and Weasley came pelting down the marble staircase, bags flying, and stopped dead at the bottom, looking left and right like a pair of lost owls.

It seemed a waste not to.

"Potter."

He turned. So did Weasley, who scowled.

"I thought you should know," said Draconia, "that the offer is closed."

Harry looked at her. "What offer?"

"The one I made you on the train. The compartment. The company." She examined her fingernails. "I've withdrawn it. I didn't want you turning up and embarrassing yourself."

"I already said no."

"Yes. And now you can't say yes. It's quite different."

He frowned. There it was, the small stubborn crease, right between his eyebrows where she'd left it. It was even better than she remembered. She felt the same warm, pleased little jolt she got when a spell worked first time.

"She's mental," Weasley told him. "Come on, we're late."

They started toward the dungeon stairs.

"Charms is on the third floor," Draconia said to the ceiling. "That way goes to the dungeons. Unless you were hoping for a nice chat with the Bloody Baron."

Harry stopped. He looked at the dungeon stairs, and then at the marble ones he'd just run down, and then, briefly, at her. He didn't say thank you. He went back up the stairs two at a time, with Weasley puffing behind him.

"You helped him," said Vivienne slowly.

It was the most she had said all morning, and Draconia did not care for it.

"I corrected him," she said. "It's not the same thing at all."

✦ ✦ ✦

She told him again on Tuesday, outside the Great Hall. "Still closed, Potter."

"Okay," said Harry.

On Wednesday she told him at the foot of a staircase that had just swung away and left him stranded on the wrong landing. "Closed. In case you'd been wondering."

"I hadn't," he called down. "You know you don't have to tell me every day?"

"I'm being courteous."

"You're being weird."

Nobody had ever called her weird. She had been called a treasure, by her grandmother, and a little madam, by a governess who was gone before lunch. She turned the word over all through History of Magic and couldn't decide what to do with it.

✦ ✦ ✦

She saw him again at break, in the courtyard. She was not looking for him. Vivienne had stopped to do up her shoe, and it would have been rude to walk on.

He was on a bench with Weasley, holding a Chocolate Frog card up to the light. Whoever was on it must have wandered out of the frame, because he turned the card over, and looked at the back, and turned it round again, and then he laughed. Out loud, at a card.

Weasley said something, and shoved him, and Potter shoved him back. He had known Weasley for three days. Already Weasley could tell him to his face that he was being an idiot, and get laughed at for it.

Draconia had known Vivienne and Greta since before she could walk. Neither of them had ever told her anything at all.

He'd been doing it all week. She had seen him stop on a staircase to watch a portrait pour itself a drink. She had seen him stare at the ceiling of the Great Hall until Weasley had to steer him round a bench. He looked at everything in the castle as though it had been put there that morning as a surprise.

Draconia had grown up with portraits that talked back. She took her own card out of her pocket, the one from the train. The frog had left her. The card had stayed. It was Agrippa, looking bored, and she watched him until he scratched his nose and strolled out of the side of the picture.

It was a bit clever, she supposed. If you'd never seen it before.

"Done," said Vivienne, who had finished with her shoe some time ago.

✦ ✦ ✦

By Wednesday night the seat needed defending.

A fifth-year with a prefect's badge tried it first. He picked the bag up by its strap and had half sat down before Greta, who had barely said a word since the train, took the bag out of his hand, waited for him to stand, and put it back. He went and sat somewhere else.

Then Vivienne put her elbow on it, and got a look.

Then, at breakfast on Thursday, Pansy leaned across the table and said, quite quietly, "It's for Potter. Isn't it?"

"It's for my bag."

"Your bag has got a lovely view of the back of his head."

Draconia said nothing. She found that she was holding her teaspoon rather tightly.

Pansy looked at her for a moment longer. She had small, sharp eyes, and people tended to forget that about her because of how much she talked.

"Well," she said. "It's a very nice bag." And she passed the marmalade, and started on something Millicent had heard about a corridor on the third floor, and never brought it up again.

The post came while they were still eating. A hundred owls poured in through the high windows, and the great eagle owl from the Manor dropped a parcel in front of Draconia that was nearly too heavy for him. There was a whole tin of sweets from her mother, with a letter four pages long asking whether the dormitory was damp. There was one line from her father, on the thick cream paper. I trust you are making the right friends.

She folded that one up small.

Across the Hall, a snowy owl had come down on the Gryffindor table. Potter's. She was a beautiful bird, far too good for him. She had nothing in her beak and nothing on her leg. She nibbled his ear, stole a corner of his toast, and flew off again.

It was the fourth morning in a row.

People always got post. Vivienne got post, and Vivienne's mother could barely spell. Draconia watched him to see what he would do about it. He didn't do anything. He stroked the owl's wing while she was there and then went back to his breakfast. He didn't even look up at the windows to see if anything else was coming.

His people must be very busy, she decided. They would have to be important, to be in charge of Harry Potter, and important people were always busy.

She pushed the tin of sweets across to Greta and didn't eat any.

✦ ✦ ✦

He got there first on Thursday.

He was coming out of the Hall after lunch as she was going in, and he didn't even slow down. "Still closed?" he said as he passed.

"Obviously," said Draconia.

She had gone ten steps before she understood what had happened. He had said her line. He had known it was coming, which meant that at some point between yesterday and today, Harry Potter had been thinking about what she was going to say to him.

She was still pink about it an hour later, in Transfiguration, where Professor McGonagall gave each of the Slytherin first-years a matchstick and told them to turn it into a needle.

By the end of the lesson most of the class had produced a matchstick. Greta's had caught fire. Pansy's had gone slightly bendy, which she insisted was progress.

Draconia's was silver from end to end and came to a point.

Professor McGonagall held it up to the window. "Good, Miss Malfoy. Very good. Only one other first-year has managed as much this week."

Draconia waited for the name. It would be a Nott, she expected, or one of the Greengrass girls. Someone who'd had wands in the house since the cradle.

"Miss Granger," said Professor McGonagall. "In Gryffindor."

Draconia went through every family she knew, and the families those families married into, all the way back to the dungeons. There were no Grangers anywhere.

"Who are the Grangers?" she asked at dinner.

"Nobody," said Pansy, with her mouth full. "It's that girl with all the hair. Millicent sat near her on the train. Her parents are dentists." She said it the way you'd say burglars. "They're Muggles. Both of them."

Draconia put her fork down. "That can't be right."

"Why not?"

Because of dinner at the Manor. Because her father had explained it over the fish course more than once, in the same easy voice he used for explaining which wine went with what. People like that came to magic late and did it badly. They slowed everybody else down. He had a word for them, a dinner word, and the guests always smiled when he used it. She had never needed it herself. There had never been one in the house.

Nobody had ever presented it to her as an opinion. It was simply how things were, like the peacocks being white.

"She'll have had help," Draconia decided. "Or a very small matchstick."

That settled it. She picked her fork back up.

But later she looked down the length of the Gryffindor table. The Granger girl was at the far end with a book propped against the milk jug. She was reading and eating at the same time and getting crumbs on the page. There was a gap on either side of her wide enough for two people.

Nobody was saving those. They were just empty.

Draconia looked at her own bag, sitting on its own stretch of bench. That was different, of course. Her seat was being saved. Granger's were just empty.

It did not occur to her that from across the Hall, the two looked exactly alike.

✦ ✦ ✦

That night she looked at her timetable for the morning, and there it was in green ink.

Friday. Double Potions. Slytherin and Gryffindor.

Two whole hours in one room, underground. Professor Snape taught it. He was an old friend of her father's and had once come to the Manor for dinner, where he had not smiled at anything. She had liked him for that.

Potter could hardly sit with his back to her for two hours in a dungeon.

She laid out her robes and brushed her hair a hundred strokes, the way her mother did it. The bag sat at the foot of her bed, packed and ready.

He hadn't come for the seat. He hadn't even noticed it. Four days she had kept it, and in those four days he had looked at her six times. She knew that because she had an unfortunate habit of counting things, which she got from her father.

She ought to give it up. She knew that.

But her father also said that you never took a good offer off the table too soon. You left it there, and you waited, and sooner or later the other person came round.

She would save the seat one more day.

Chapter Two: The Remembrall

It took Draconia exactly one week to discover the best thing about Hogwarts.

It wasn't the moving staircases, which were rude, or the ghosts, who were ruder. It wasn't even the Slytherin common room, though she approved of it: long and low and green-lit, with the lake pressing against the windows and a fire that nobody ever had to be told to light.

The best thing about Hogwarts was double Potions on Friday morning.

Because Professor Snape hated Harry Potter.

He didn't hide it, either. He swept into the dungeon like a bat that had been personally insulted, took the register, and stopped at Harry's name with a silky little pause that made every Slytherin in the room sit up straight. Then he asked Harry three questions in a row that nobody in first year could possibly have answered.

Harry didn't know the first. Or the second. Or the third.

Across the room, Hermione Granger's hand was stretched so high she was nearly standing on her chair. Snape ignored her completely. Draconia had never liked a teacher so much in her life.

"Clearly," Snape said, "fame isn't everything."

The Slytherins laughed. Draconia laughed loudest, because that was what you did.

But she was watching Harry while she did it.

He didn't go red, the way Neville Longbottom did when anyone so much as looked at him. He went very still. His jaw set. And there it was: that small, stubborn crease between his brows, the same one from the train, as if he'd decided that whatever Snape threw at him, he was simply going to stand there and take it.

There, thought Draconia, with deep satisfaction. That face.

She told herself she enjoyed it because it meant he was suffering.

She took extra care, for the rest of the lesson, to make sure he suffered as often as possible.

"Potter," she said sweetly, when Snape had turned his back, "your cauldron's smoking."

It wasn't. He looked anyway. Then he looked at her, and the crease came back, sharper.

She went back to her own potion, humming.

✦ ✦ ✦

The flying lesson was on Thursday afternoon, on a stretch of flat lawn below the castle, and Draconia had been looking forward to it for three days.

She had been flying since she was five. There had been a broom in the Manor gardens for as long as she could remember, and a patient, terrified house-elf whose job was to stand underneath it. She could loop the east tower and hang upside down by her knees and outrun the peacocks, and nobody at this school had ever seen her do any of it.

They were about to.

Twenty brooms lay in two neat lines on the grass. Slytherins on one side, Gryffindors on the other. Madam Hooch, who had short grey hair and yellow eyes like a hawk, strode up and down between them.

"Right hand over your broom," she barked. "And say up!"

"Up," said Draconia, and her broom leapt into her palm as if it had been waiting all morning.

Of course it did.

She glanced across the line, ready to enjoy the Gryffindors struggling. Ron Weasley's broom had rolled over and hit him in the shin. Hermione Granger's was lying perfectly still on the grass while she glared at it like it had failed a test. Neville Longbottom's was trembling as badly as he was.

And Harry Potter's broom had jumped straight into his hand on the very first try.

He looked at it with an expression of pure, delighted surprise, as if nobody had ever given him a present before.

Draconia looked away quickly. It was an extremely irritating expression.

She was still being irritated about it when Neville Longbottom, too nervous to wait for the whistle, kicked off hard, shot straight up into the air like a cork out of a bottle, and fell off.

It happened very fast. There was a thud, and a nasty crack, and then Neville was lying face down on the grass, and Madam Hooch was bending over him, white-lipped.

"Broken wrist," she muttered. "Up you get, boy, it's all right." She turned to the rest of them, hauling Neville upright. "I'm taking him to the hospital wing. Nobody moves. Brooms stay on the ground, or you'll be out of Hogwarts before you can say Quidditch. Understood?"

They understood.

They watched her march Neville away across the lawn, holding his wrist to his chest, until they were two small figures at the castle doors.

Then Draconia saw it.

Lying in the grass where Neville had fallen was a small glass ball, about the size of a large marble. It was full of white smoke. She knew what it was: a Remembrall. The smoke turned red when you'd forgotten something. Neville's grandmother had sent it at breakfast that week, and the whole Great Hall had watched it glow scarlet in his hand while he tried to work out what he'd forgotten.

She bent and picked it up.

"Look," she said, holding it up to the light. "Longbottom forgot his dignity."

The Slytherins laughed. Pansy laughed so hard she had to hold onto Millicent.

"Give that here, Malfoy."

She didn't need to turn around. She knew the voice. She turned around anyway, slowly, so he'd have to wait.

Harry was standing in front of the Gryffindor line. Everybody had gone quiet.

Oh, she thought. Hello.

There it was already. The crease.

"I don't think I will," said Draconia pleasantly. "I think I'll leave it somewhere for Longbottom to find. Somewhere nice." She tilted her head. "Like the top of a tree."

"Give it here!"

But Draconia had already swung her leg over her broom and pushed off.

She had not lied about the flying. She rose smooth and fast, her hair streaming behind her, up past the treetops, until the lawn was a green handkerchief below and her classmates were little upturned faces. She hovered there, level with the highest branches of an old oak, and let the Remembrall roll lazily from one palm to the other.

It was glorious. Nobody down there could touch her.

"Come and get it, Potter," she called. "If you can."

"Harry, no!" Hermione Granger's voice, sharp with panic. "Madam Hooch told us not to move! You'll get us all into trouble!"

Harry ignored her.

He mounted his broom. He kicked hard against the ground.

And he rose.

Draconia had expected him to wobble. He'd grown up with Muggles; he'd probably never so much as seen a broom before this morning. She had expected him to lurch and flail and, ideally, to fall off in front of everyone, like Longbottom.

He didn't.

He shot up through the air as if he'd been born doing it. He turned the broom with his knees, easily, without even seeming to think about it, and came to a stop level with her. His hair was wilder than ever. His eyes behind his glasses were bright. And there was something in his face she'd never seen on it before, not in the robe shop, not on the train, not in Potions.

He was happy.

He flies like it's the first thing in his life that's ever been his, she thought, and then, immediately, furiously: That is deeply, deeply unfair.

"Give it here," Harry said, "or I'll knock you off that broom."

"Oh, will you?"

She tried to sound bored. It came out breathless, which was the altitude.

He leaned forward. His broom leapt at her like a javelin.

She only just swerved in time. He turned sharply and came back around, and a wild cheer went up from the Gryffindors below, and for the first time in her life, Draconia Malfoy felt the ground shift under her while her feet weren't even on it.

He was good.

He was better than good. He was better than her.

She did not have a plan for this.

So she did the only thing she could think of, the thing her father would have done: she made it someone else's problem.

"Catch it if you can, then!" she shouted, and she threw the Remembrall as high and as far as she could.

It glittered in the sun, arcing up, then down, toward the ground.

And Harry went after it.

He didn't hesitate. He simply tipped his broom handle down and dived, straight at the ground, faster and faster. Below, somebody screamed.

Draconia's stomach dropped with him.

For one horrible second, she didn't care about the Remembrall, or Longbottom, or winning, or anything at all. She only saw how fast he was going and how close the ground was, and her hand clenched so hard around her broom handle that the knots of the wood bit into her palm.

Pull up, she thought. Pull up, pull up, you idiot, PULL UP...

A foot from the grass, his hand closed around the glass ball.

He pulled out of the dive so smoothly it looked easy, and toppled gently off onto the lawn, and held up the Remembrall, whole and unbroken.

The Gryffindors went mad.

Draconia unclenched her hand. There were little white marks on her palm where the wood had pressed. She stared at them.

I was worried, she realized, with growing horror. I was worried about Potter.

No. No, she wasn't. She'd been worried that if he broke his neck, she'd be blamed. That was all. That was completely reasonable.

She was still telling herself so, drifting down toward the lawn with her chin as high as she could make it, when a voice rang out across the grass.

"HARRY POTTER!"

Professor McGonagall was running toward them.

Draconia landed, very lightly, and kept her face perfectly calm. Inside, something small and gleeful unfurled. Harry was pale. Professor McGonagall's glasses were flashing. She was so shocked she could barely speak. Never, she was saying, and in all my time at Hogwarts, and how dare you, and could have broken your neck.

Yes, thought Draconia. Expelled. He's going to be expelled.

"Follow me, Potter. Now."

Harry followed her, trudging, not looking back.

The Slytherins erupted in delighted whispers. Pansy clutched Draconia's arm. "He's finished," she breathed. "Expelled in his first week. Oh, Draconia, that was brilliant."

"Yes," said Draconia. "Wasn't it."

She watched Harry's small figure walk away beside Professor McGonagall, up the stone steps, into the shadow of the great front doors, and it didn't feel brilliant at all.

It felt like the moment on the train when her hand had been hanging in the air.

Good, she told herself firmly. Good. That's what he gets.

But she didn't laugh.

✦ ✦ ✦

He was not expelled.

She found this out at dinner, the way the whole school found it out, because news at Hogwarts traveled faster than owls. By the time the pudding appeared, the story had been told at every table, and it got a little bigger each time. By the time it reached the Slytherin end, Harry had caught the Remembrall with his teeth.

"Seeker?" said Draconia.

"Seeker," Pansy confirmed miserably. "For Gryffindor. McGonagall took him straight to their Quidditch captain. Millicent heard it from a Hufflepuff who heard it from one of the Weasley twins."

"But first-years aren't allowed on the house teams. First-years aren't even allowed to own brooms." Draconia put down her fork. "That's the rule."

"They're bending it."

"For him?"

"He's the youngest house player in a century," said Vivienne, from further down the bench. Everybody turned to look at her. She didn't usually speak. She went pink and looked at her plate.

Draconia sat very still.

All her life, rules had bent for her. The other way had simply never occurred to her: that there might be a world where rules bent for someone else, while she stood right there, and nobody even thought to ask her.

She had never been told no.

But she'd just been told not you.

It turned out that was worse.

She looked down the length of the Great Hall to the Gryffindor table, where Harry was sitting between Ron Weasley and a pair of whooping red-haired twins who kept thumping him on the back. He was grinning. He looked like he couldn't quite believe it.

He glanced up. His eyes found hers across the Hall.

And he smiled at her.

It wasn't a nice smile. It wasn't a mean one, either. It was a small, crooked, private smile that said, plainly as words, you did this.

Which she had.

Draconia's face went hot from her collar to her hair. She put her fork down so hard it rang against the plate.

"I'm going over there," she announced.

"Why?" said Pansy.

"To challenge him," said Draconia, standing, "to a duel."

✦ ✦ ✦

The Gryffindor table went quiet as she approached, flanked by Vivienne and Greta. Harry looked up. Ron Weasley put down his drumstick.

"Enjoying your last meal, Potter?" she said. "I'd make the most of it."

"I'm not going anywhere," said Harry. "In fact, I'm staying for good. Didn't you hear?"

"I heard." She lifted her chin. "Very brave of you, flying when there's a teacher around to catch you. I wonder how brave you'd be without one."

The crease appeared. There it is, thought Draconia. Hello again.

"Is that a challenge?"

"A wizard's duel. Tonight. Midnight." She smiled. "Wands only. No contact. Unless you're scared."

"He's not scared," said Ron, before Harry could open his mouth. "I'm his second. Who's yours?"

Draconia looked at Vivienne and Greta. She looked back at Ron. "Vivienne," she said, choosing at random. "The trophy room. It's always unlocked."

"Fine," said Harry.

"Fine," said Draconia.

She turned and walked back to the Slytherin table with every eye in the Great Hall on her, and sat down, and ate an entire treacle tart very slowly, and did not look at the Gryffindor table again for the rest of the meal.

"You're not actually going," said Pansy. It wasn't a question.

"Of course not," said Draconia. "I'm going to tell Filch."

✦ ✦ ✦

It was the right plan. It was a Malfoy plan. Potter would creep out of bed in the middle of the night, into the trophy room, and find, instead of a duel, the caretaker. Detention. Points from Gryffindor. Perhaps, if she was very lucky, his new position on the Quidditch team quietly withdrawn.

She told Filch after dinner, very sweetly, in the voice she used on shopkeepers. He practically licked his lips.

Then she went to bed.

And she lay there.

It was nearly half past eleven. Then it was a quarter to twelve. The lake moved green and black against the dormitory window, and Pansy snored gently in the next bed, and Draconia lay flat on her back with her eyes wide open, staring at her bed hangings.

He'll get caught, she thought.

Good.

It was twelve o'clock.

He's probably creeping down the corridor right now. With Weasley. And that ridiculous hair.

Five past.

Filch will be waiting. He'll grab him by the collar. Serves him right.

Ten past.

What if it's not Filch? What if it's Snape?

She turned over.

He'll look at Snape the way he did in Potions, she thought. With the jaw. And the crease.

She turned back.

He's not even going to know it was me. He'll think I got scared. He'll think I was too scared to come.

That thought was so intolerable that she sat bolt upright in bed.

Then she lay back down, because she was not, under any circumstances, going to go down to the trophy room at midnight to explain to Harry Potter that she hadn't been scared, she'd simply betrayed him. That would be insane.

She didn't sleep until nearly two.

✦ ✦ ✦

He was at breakfast the next morning.

Not in detention. Not in trouble. Not even tired. He was sitting at the Gryffindor table eating toast, looking extremely pleased with himself about something, and Ron Weasley kept leaning over to whisper to him and then glancing up at the ceiling as if he'd seen a ghost.

Draconia stared at him over her porridge.

It wasn't possible. Filch had been waiting. Filch had promised.

Harry looked up. He saw her staring. And after a moment, he got up from the Gryffindor table, and walked the whole length of the Hall, and stopped beside her.

The Slytherins went quiet.

"You didn't turn up," he said.

"I had better things to do."

"Like telling Filch?"

She opened her mouth. She closed it.

He'd worked it out. Of course he had. He'd looked at her and worked it out, the same way he'd looked at her in Madam Malkin's and simply seen her, as if she were made of glass.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, with great dignity.

"Yeah, you do." He didn't sound angry. That was the worst part. He sounded almost amused. "It's fine. Nearly got us caught, but it's fine." He leaned a little closer, just enough that only she could hear. "Next time you want to see me at midnight, Malfoy, you could just ask."

Then he walked back to his own table.

Draconia sat perfectly still.

Her face was on fire. Her ears were on fire. The porridge was probably on fire.

"What did he say?" Pansy hissed. "What did he say?"

"Nothing," said Draconia.

"You've gone completely red!"

"It's the porridge," said Draconia. "It's very hot."

She pushed the bowl away. Across the Hall, Harry was laughing at something Ron had said, and he didn't look over at her again, not once, which was the most infuriating thing he had ever done.

Ask, she thought. Ask. As if a Malfoy ever asked for anything.

She was offered things.

That was the rule.

✦ ✦ ✦

She wrote to her father that evening, in her very best handwriting, on the thick cream paper with the family crest.

Dear Father,

Harry Potter has been made Seeker for Gryffindor. He is a first-year. They broke the rule for him. It was not fair and nobody asked me.

I would like a broom. The best one. And I would like to be Seeker for Slytherin next year, and I would like to beat him.

Please.

She looked at that last word for a long time. She had never written it to her father before. She nearly crossed it out.

In the end, she left it.

His reply came three days later, at breakfast, borne by the great eagle owl, and it was only one line long.

We'll see.

Draconia read it twice, folded it very small, and tucked it into the pocket of her robes, where she could feel it all day.

In Malfoy Manor, we'll see meant yes.

It just took a little longer.

End of Chapter Two

Chapter 2.1: The Right Sort of Friends

Year One. Halloween.

By the last week of October, the bag had been on the bench for eight weeks.

She had put it there on the first morning of term, on the seat beside her own, to keep the place for Harry Potter. He had turned down her hand on the train, and she had decided to give him the chance to change his mind. He had not taken it. He had not even noticed.

Nobody else tried to sit there any more. The Slytherin table had simply grown round the bag, the way a tree grows round a railing. Draconia still took the end place nearest the doors every morning. Potter still came in at ten past eight and sat down at the Gryffindor table, on the far side of the Hall, with his back to her. She had got so used to both things that she had almost stopped noticing the second one.

Almost.

It was her father's letter that started the trouble. He wrote once a week, one line, on the thick cream paper. This week's said what the very first one had said. I trust you are making the right friends.

She read it over her porridge, and then she looked up at her friends.

Vivienne Crabbe was eating a sausage. Greta Goyle was eating two. They had sat on either side of her at every meal since the first of September, and before that at every birthday party since she was three. Their fathers did whatever her father told them, and it had seemed only tidy for the daughters to match.

They were the right friends. She knew that. They had been chosen very carefully, by somebody else.

"Tell me I'm wrong about something," said Draconia.

Vivienne stopped chewing. "What?"

"Anything. I say a great many things. One of them must have been wrong by now. Tell me which."

Vivienne looked at Greta. Greta looked at her plate.

"You're not wrong," said Vivienne.

"About anything?"

"No."

"On Tuesday I said that Professor Binns was alive."

Professor Binns taught History of Magic. He had died in his armchair forty years ago, and had got up the next morning and gone to class without his body. He floated in through the blackboard.

"He might be," said Vivienne loyally.

Greta was thinking. You could always tell, with Greta. It took a while, and it showed.

"Your shoelace is undone," she said at last.

It wasn't the same thing. A shoelace was not an opinion. But it was the nearest anybody had come, and Draconia bent down and tied it.

Across the table, Pansy Parkinson had watched the whole of this over the rim of her goblet. Pansy shared their dormitory. She had sat opposite for eight weeks, because there was a bag in the place beside. She talked from the moment she sat down until the moment she got up, about who had said what to whom, and Draconia had learned to let it run past her like the lake past the dormitory window.

Pansy wasn't saying anything now. That was unusual enough to notice.

✦ ✦ ✦

The news came down from the Charms classroom at lunchtime.

The Gryffindor first-years had spent the morning trying to make a feather float. Only one of them had managed it: Hermione Granger, the girl with all the hair, whose parents were Muggles. That meant they had no magic at all. They were dentists. And she had still done it at the first attempt, and then told Ron Weasley how he was doing it wrong.

Weasley had said something about her afterwards, in the corridor. Granger had been in the girls' bathroom ever since, crying.

"He said she's a nightmare," Pansy reported happily. "He said it's no wonder nobody can stand her. And she heard every word. Millicent was right behind them."

"So even a Weasley can see it," said Draconia.

It was the correct thing to say. It was what her father would have said. He held that magic belonged to the old wizarding families, and that people like Granger had wandered into a world that was not theirs. Sooner or later, he said, even the fools who welcomed them would notice. Draconia waited for it to feel as correct as it sounded.

Instead she thought of Granger at the far end of the Gryffindor table, with a book propped against the milk jug and an empty place on either side of her. Nobody had put a bag on those.

Granger had no Vivienne and no Greta. Granger had nobody sitting next to her at all, chosen or otherwise. It ought to have been more satisfying than it was.

"She'll have cried herself out by the feast," said Draconia, and helped herself to potatoes.

She hadn't.

✦ ✦ ✦

There were a thousand live bats in the Great Hall for the Halloween feast, and pumpkins the size of carriages. Draconia had just taken a baked potato when one of the teachers came running in.

It was Professor Quirrell, a pale, twitchy young man who taught Defence Against the Dark Arts and stammered through every lesson. His turban was crooked. He got as far as the top table, gasped, "Troll... in the dungeons... thought you ought to know," and fell flat on his face.

Draconia had seen a troll once, in a book. They were twelve feet tall and as stupid as a boulder, and they could pull a grown wizard in half without really meaning to.

It took several purple firecrackers from the Headmaster's wand to get silence.

"Prefects," said Professor Dumbledore. "Lead your houses back to the dormitories. At once."

The prefects were older students with silver badges, whose job was to be obeyed. The Slytherin one was the fifth-year who had once tried to move her bag. He had the whole house halfway across the entrance hall at a march before Draconia stopped walking.

"Excuse me," she said. "Where is our dormitory?"

The Gryffindors slept at the top of a tower. The Slytherins slept under the lake.

"In the dung..." The prefect stopped too.

"Yes," said Draconia. "That's what I thought."

So the whole of Slytherin was put in a chamber off the entrance hall instead, crammed in shoulder to shoulder, with the door left open and the prefect guarding it. He looked as though he would have liked to have thought of it himself.

From where she stood she could see the marble staircase, and the Gryffindors going up it in a long, noisy line.

She counted the first-years. She couldn't help it. She had her father's habit of counting things, and she had been counting one of them in particular for eight weeks.

Longbottom. Finnigan. Thomas. The two girls who giggled.

No Weasley. No Potter.

She found them at once, because she always did. They had dropped out of the back of the line beside a suit of armour. Potter had Weasley by the sleeve and was saying something very fast. Weasley's ears had gone red. Then both of them ducked their heads and ran. Not up the stairs. Along the side corridor, the one that led to the girls' bathroom.

Granger.

Granger had not been at the feast. Granger did not know there was a troll.

And Harry Potter, who didn't even like her, whose own friend had made her cry, was going to fetch her out from under it.

Something went through Draconia like cold water. It was not admiration. She was quite certain about that. It was much more like fury. He had grown up with Muggles. He had been a wizard for three months. He would walk straight up to a twelve-foot troll with that frown on his face, as though frowning were a plan.

"I'm going after them," she said.

"No," said Pansy.

Draconia turned round.

Pansy was right behind her. Her small sharp eyes were on the corridor where the boys had gone. She had seen it too.

"I beg your pardon?"

"No. It's a stupid idea." Pansy kept her voice low and quick. "You know four spells, and one of them's for turning matchsticks into needles. It's a troll. And if you go running after Harry Potter in front of the whole house, you will never live it down. Not ever. They'll still be saying it when we're seventh-years, and I'll have to listen to all of it."

Nobody at the Manor had ever said that word to her. Not the house-elves, not the tutors, not the peacocks on the lawn. Only one person in her whole life had, on a train, and she had been trying to make him pay for it ever since.

It was extraordinary how different it sounded from somebody who was on her side.

She went anyway. She got two steps.

"Malfoy." The prefect had turned. "Where do you think you're going?"

Draconia opened her mouth, and found that there was nothing in it.

"She's going to be sick," said Pansy loudly. She had hold of Draconia's elbow. "It's the pumpkin. She's gone green, look at her. I'm taking her to sit down, unless you'd like it on your shoes."

The prefect looked at Draconia's face, which had in fact turned a peculiar colour, and stepped back.

Pansy sat her on a bench against the wall, and sat down next to her. Close. Nobody ever sat that close to Draconia without being told to.

"Professor Snape went that way," Pansy said under her breath. "I saw him. And Professor McGonagall straight after. And Quirrell got up off that floor rather quickly for someone who'd fainted, if you ask me. That's three teachers. You like counting. Count those."

Draconia counted them. It helped a little.

"I wasn't going after Potter," she said.

"Of course not. You were going to be sick."

"I don't even like him."

"No," Pansy agreed. "You've only looked at the back of his head every morning for two months."

Draconia turned and stared at her. Pansy looked back, perfectly calm.

"You talk all the time," said Draconia slowly. "You never stop. I didn't think you noticed anything."

"Nobody does." Pansy shrugged. "People stop listening after the first minute. Then they forget I've got eyes. It's very useful."

"Why did you stop me?"

Pansy thought about it. For once she took her time.

"Because you'd have looked ridiculous," she said. "And I'd have minded."

✦ ✦ ✦

They heard at midnight, in the common room, the way Slytherin heard everything: late, and third-hand.

Potter and Weasley had knocked out a full-grown mountain troll in the girls' bathroom, with its own club. Professor McGonagall, who was head of Gryffindor and not known for giving anything away, had awarded them five house points each. And Granger had stood in front of her and said that it was all her own fault. She had gone looking for the troll herself, she said, and the boys had only come to save her.

"But that isn't true," said Draconia. "She was crying in there. She didn't go looking for anything."

"So she lied," said Pansy. "To a teacher's face. To keep them out of trouble."

Draconia sat back in her chair.

Gryffindors were supposed to be the brave ones. Slytherins were supposed to be the clever ones, the ones who knew when a good lie would serve their own people better than the truth. She would have done precisely what Granger had done. Any Slytherin would.

It was really very annoying of Granger.

✦ ✦ ✦

They came in to breakfast together.

Not Potter and Weasley, with Granger trailing behind. The three of them, side by side, and Granger in the middle. Weasley was telling the story with both arms. Potter was laughing. Granger was pretending to disapprove and not managing it. They sat down at the Gryffindor table all in a row, and the gaps on either side of Hermione Granger were gone, just like that, as if they had never been there.

Draconia watched.

Nobody had arranged it. Weasley's father did not work for Potter's. Nobody's mother had written to anybody's mother. Yesterday the girl had been crying in a bathroom because of them, and today she was passing Weasley the marmalade, and all it had taken was one troll.

They had picked each other.

She looked at Vivienne on her left, and at Greta on her right.

"Greta," she said. "If your father didn't work for mine. Would you still sit here?"

Vivienne choked on her pumpkin juice. Greta put down her fork and thought. It took a long time. The toast went cold.

"I don't know," Greta said finally. She had gone pink. "Nobody ever asked me where I wanted to sit."

It was not the answer Draconia had wanted. It was also, she suspected, the first true thing Greta Goyle had ever said to her. She did not know where to put it, so she kept it.

Pansy came in late, as she always did, already talking. It was something about Millicent's cat and a Hufflepuff. She got as far as the end of the table and stopped, the way she had stopped every morning for eight weeks, to go round to the other side.

Draconia picked up the bag.

It was black dragonhide with a silver clasp, and it had had the best seat at the best table for two months. Potter had never once looked at it. She put it on the floor, under the bench, by her feet.

She did not say thank you. She had never said it to anybody and meant it, and she wasn't sure how it was done.

Pansy looked at the empty place. Then she looked at Draconia, for about a second, with those small sharp eyes.

Then she sat down, and reached for the toast, and went on about the cat as though nothing had happened at all. Neither of them ever mentioned it.

It was rather nice, Draconia found, having somebody there. The bag had never passed the marmalade.

✦ ✦ ✦

She did not think of it as giving up. The offer had been a very good one, and he'd had eight weeks. She had simply found a better use for the bench.

There were other ways to make Harry Potter look at her.

"I hear you fought a troll, Potter," she said outside the Great Hall, at five to nine. "Over a girl. How gallant. Did it faint when it smelled your hair?"

He turned round. There it was: the small, stubborn crease he got between his eyebrows whenever she annoyed him. She had been collecting it since the train.

"At least I've got friends worth fighting one for," said Harry.

He went off up the marble staircase with the other two. He hadn't meant anything by it. He never did. That was the trouble with him.

Draconia stood where she was.

"He's wrong," said a voice at her elbow. Pansy had not even looked up from her timetable. "Obviously. Come on. We've got Transfiguration."

That night Draconia wrote to her father. I have made a friend. Her name is Pansy Parkinson.

The answer came on Thursday. The Parkinsons are acceptable.

She read it twice, as she read everything of his.

It was only afterwards that she noticed something strange. She had not asked for his permission. She had only told him. And in all the days she had waited for the owl, she had not once worried about whether he would approve.

She would have kept Pansy either way. She had never thought a thing like that before.

She folded the letter up small, and passed Pansy the marmalade.

Chapter 2.2: The Forest

Year One. The end of May.

It began with a dragon, which Draconia took as a personal insult.

She had not been following Potter. She had been taking the air, at dusk, on the lawn, in the general direction of Hagrid's hut, on four separate evenings.

Hagrid was the school gamekeeper. He was twice the size of a normal man, he lived in a wooden hut at the edge of the grounds, and he was Harry Potter's friend. Potter had been going down there far too often lately. He kept coming back with scorch marks on his sleeves.

On the fourth evening she looked through a gap in the curtains. There was a black thing the size of a kettle on the kitchen table. It sneezed, and set fire to Hagrid's beard.

It was a baby dragon. Potter was in there. So was Granger. They were both laughing.

Draconia stood in the wet grass with her nose an inch from the glass. She was named for a dragon. There had been one on her nursery ceiling, picked out in silver stars. And it was Harry Potter, who had not known dragons existed until last summer, who got to sit at a table with a real one.

It was just like the broom. In his first week he had been put on the Quidditch team, against the rules, without even asking. Things simply came to him.

She knew what a Malfoy did with a secret like this. Keeping a dragon was against the law. You carried the secret to a teacher, quietly, and you let the teacher do the rest.

But she had tried that already. In September she had challenged Potter to a duel at midnight, and then sent the caretaker to catch him instead of going herself. Potter had got away. Worse, he had worked out what she'd done. The next morning he had leaned over her porridge and said, Next time you want to see me at midnight, Malfoy, you could just ask.

She had thought about that sentence every day since. She knew, because she had counted.

She was not going to ask. But this time she would not send somebody else either.

Finding out the plan was easy. The dragon had bitten Ron Weasley, and he was in the hospital wing with a hand swollen to the size of a melon, telling everyone it was a dog. Weasley did not know how to whisper. By Thursday she had the whole of it. They were sending the dragon away, to friends of Weasley's older brother. Saturday. Midnight. The top of the Astronomy Tower, the tallest tower in the castle.

So she would be there. She would be standing at the top of the stairs when Potter arrived with his illegal dragon. He would see her face, and he would know exactly who had caught him. Nobody was ever going to say that she had been too scared to come.

She got as far as the bottom of the tower.

"Miss Malfoy."

It was Professor McGonagall, the strictest teacher in the school. She was wearing a tartan dressing gown and a hairnet. Neither of them made her any less frightening.

"Potter's coming, Professor! He's got a dragon!"

"What utter rubbish. How dare you tell such lies. Twenty points from Slytherin, and a detention."

"But he's..."

"Bed, Miss Malfoy."

She was marched down to the dungeons like a parcel. It was the worst night of her life.

Then came breakfast, and it got better.

Students were not allowed out of bed at night. That was the rule she had broken, and Potter had broken it too. The caretaker had caught him and Granger coming down from the tower twenty minutes after her. The dragon was already gone by then, so they could not even explain what they had been doing up there. And he had caught a third Gryffindor, Neville Longbottom, who had been wandering the corridors trying to warn them.

Professor McGonagall was head of Gryffindor, and she was harder on her own house than on anyone else's. She took fifty points from each of them. Three students, fifty points each. A hundred and fifty points, gone in one night.

The four houses competed all year for points, and Gryffindor had been winning. Now they were in last place. Everybody knew whose idea the tower had been. The whole school blamed Harry Potter, and stopped speaking to him.

Draconia had done that from the bottom of a staircase, in her dressing gown. She waited to feel pleased about it.

It took nearly a week, and even then it wasn't right. He went about the castle with his head down. When she said, "Lost something, Potter? About a hundred and fifty of them?", he did not frown at her. He just walked past.

She had spent all year trying to make him frown. It was the only way she knew to make him look at her. She found that she did not like it at all when he stopped.

✦ ✦ ✦

The note came at breakfast. Your detention will take place at eleven o'clock tonight. Meet Mr. Filch in the entrance hall.

She had expected to write lines. Instead Filch, the caretaker, led them out through the front doors and across the dark lawn with a lamp. There were four of them: Potter, Granger, Longbottom, who was sniffing already, and herself.

There was a light in the window of Hagrid's hut. In front of it stood a very large shape, holding a crossbow.

Behind him were the trees. The Forbidden Forest was the one place no student was ever allowed to go. Everybody knew why. There were things living in it.

"The Forest?" Draconia had stopped walking. "We can't go in there at night. There are werewolves. And this is servants' work. When my father hears..."

"He'll tell yeh that's how it is at Hogwarts," said Hagrid. "Yeh've done wrong, an' now yeh pay fer it. Or yeh can go back up ter the castle an' pack yer trunk."

Nobody had spoken to her like that since Potter himself, in a robe shop, on the day they met. She looked at the crossbow, and at the castle, and stayed where she was.

Hagrid held the lamp low over the path. Something on the ground shone silver, as if the moon had been spilt.

"Unicorn blood," he said. "There's one in there bin hurt bad by summat. Second time in a week. We're gonna find the poor thing. If yeh find it, send up green sparks with yer wand. If yeh get inter trouble, send up red, an' we'll all come runnin'."

"I want the dog," said Draconia. Hagrid's dog, Fang, was the size of a small pony and had a great many teeth.

"All righ'. But I warn yeh, he's a coward."

She went left with Longbottom and the dog. Potter and Granger went right with Hagrid. Their lamp got smaller and smaller between the trees, and then it was gone.

She lasted ten minutes. It was very dark. Longbottom jumped at every twig. She was frightened, and she hated being frightened, and the only thing in that whole Forest that she knew how to do was scare Neville Longbottom. So she dropped behind him, and grabbed his shoulders, and said, "Boo."

He screamed. Then he sent up red sparks.

Hagrid came crashing through the trees. He was furious. Then he changed the groups. "Neville, yeh stay with me an' Hermione. Harry, you go with Fang an' this one. Sorry," he added to Potter, not nearly quietly enough, "but she'll have a harder job frightenin' you."

Draconia looked at a tree. It had been a stupid thing to do.

She had not planned it. But it had put her with Potter, and she found that she did not mind that at all.

✦ ✦ ✦

They walked for a long time. Potter carried the lamp.

"This is your fault," said Draconia.

"You followed me up a tower."

"I was catching you."

"You caught McGonagall."

"I came," she said. "Myself. In person. You told me to ask. I don't ask. So I came. I should like that noted."

He looked at her sideways. "Noted."

"If you had sat with us on the train," she went on, "you would be in bed now. Slytherins don't get caught with dragons. We'd have found you a better class of secret."

"If I'd sat with you, I'd have had to listen to you all year."

"You've listened to me all year anyway."

"Yeah," said Harry. "I'd noticed."

It was not a compliment. She kept it anyway.

The path got narrower. The silver splashes on the ground were getting bigger. She noticed that she was walking closer to the lamp than she needed to, and that her hand had taken hold of Fang's collar.

"It'll be badly hurt, with that much blood," she said. "And unicorns are almost impossible to catch. We've got a tapestry of one at home. There's a hair from one in my wand."

Every wand had something magical at its heart. Hers was a single unicorn hair.

"Mine's a phoenix feather," said Harry.

"Of course it is," said Draconia bitterly. Phoenixes were rarer.

They walked on. An owl called somewhere. To fill the silence she said, "I suppose Granger thinks she'll find it first. She thinks if she reads enough books she'll turn into one of us."

Potter stopped. The lamp stopped with him.

He was frowning. But it was not the small annoyed frown she liked. It was colder than that.

"One of who?"

"People who belong here. Families who have always had magic."

"I haven't always been here. I didn't know any of this existed till last July."

"That's different."

"How?"

She opened her mouth.

At home it was easy. Her father said it all the time. Magic belonged to the old families. People born to Muggles, like Granger, were outsiders. He could explain it in one sentence over dinner, and the whole table would nod.

But she was not at the dinner table. She was in the dark, with a boy holding a lamp, who had grown up with Muggles himself. And she could not find her father's sentence anywhere. She had never had to explain it before. She had only ever had to agree.

"It just is," she said.

"That's not a reason."

"It isn't supposed to be a reason. It's how things are."

"Okay," said Harry. It was the flat kind of okay that meant he had stopped listening. He walked on, and she had to hurry to stay in the light.

She did not change her mind that night. But it was the first time anybody had asked her why, and it bothered her that she had not had an answer.

✦ ✦ ✦

Fang stopped first. He whined, low in his throat, and would not go forward.

There was a clearing ahead, with moonlight in it. Something white lay on the ground.

It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it was dead.

The unicorn's long legs stuck out at angles, the way it had fallen. Its mane was spread over the dark leaves like spilt pearls. Draconia had never seen a dead thing before, except on a dinner plate. Her hand went into her pocket and closed round her wand, with its one unicorn hair inside.

Then a bush shook on the far side of the clearing. Something in a hooded cloak came out of the shadows. It was crawling, like a thing that had forgotten how to walk. It bent its head to the wound in the unicorn's side, and began to drink the blood.

Draconia screamed. She did not decide to. Fang ran, and she was holding his collar, and then she was running too. Brambles. Roots. Her own breath. Ten steps, twenty, thirty.

Then she noticed how dark it was.

The lamp was not with her. The lamp was back in the clearing. Potter had it. And Potter had not run.

She stopped so suddenly that she nearly fell. Fang went on without her.

Her father had a rule for everything. She tried to think what the rule was for this. A Malfoy looks after herself first. That was probably it.

It did not feel right. He was back there alone.

She turned round.

Through the trees she could see the lamp, lying on its side in the leaves. Potter was on his knees next to it, with both hands pressed to the scar on his forehead, as if it hurt. The hooded thing had got up from the unicorn. It was moving towards him. Silver blood was dripping from the place where its face should have been.

She was eleven. She knew six spells. Four of them were for matchsticks and feathers. She could not fight it.

But she could make a noise.

She pointed her wand straight up and sent red sparks tearing through the branches, the way Hagrid had told them. Then she screamed, as loudly as she had ever screamed in her life.

"HAGRID! HAGRID!"

She had called him a servant once. She had said he was beneath her. Now she screamed his name four more times, because he was the only one who could help.

There was a sound of hooves. Something huge came over the bushes in one leap. It was a centaur, a man down to the waist and a horse below it. Centaurs lived deep in the Forest. It charged straight at the hooded thing, rearing up. The thing fled. There was a swirl of black cloak, and then nothing.

It was very quiet.

Draconia walked back into the clearing. Her legs were shaking. Her wand was still pointing at the sky. Potter was getting to his feet. His face was as white as paper.

The centaur looked down at him. "You are the Potter boy. The Forest is not safe tonight, and least of all for you. Can you ride?" Then he turned his head and looked at the wand in her fist. "The sparks were yours."

"Somebody had to," said Draconia.

"It is not you that it wants, child. Stay in the light. Hagrid is close."

Potter climbed up onto the centaur's back. From up there, with his glasses crooked, he looked down at her.

"You came back," he said.

It was true. She had run away, and then she had come back. But a Malfoy did not admit to running away.

"I didn't go anywhere," she said. "I was fetching help. It's not the same thing at all."

He did not frown. He looked at her for a moment, as if he was working something out. Then the centaur turned, and they were gone.

She was alone in the clearing, with the lamp and the dead unicorn.

She had meant to stand still and wait. Instead she knelt down in the leaves and put her hand on its neck. It was still warm. She thought about the hair in her wand, and wondered if her wand could feel that one of its own kind had died.

Her eyes were wet. She told herself it was the smoke from the lamp.

She took her hand away and stood up when she heard Hagrid coming. Nobody was going to find a Malfoy crying over an animal.

He crashed into the clearing with Granger and Longbottom behind him. He saw the unicorn, and his whole enormous face crumpled. Then he saw her, and the wand.

"You sent them sparks up?"

"Evidently."

"Where's Harry?"

"A centaur took him. He's perfectly all right. He didn't even scream." She heard what she had just said, and added quickly, "I screamed. On purpose. So that somebody would hear."

Hagrid looked at her for a long moment.

"That was well done," he said gruffly.

"I didn't do it for you."

"Never said yeh did."

Granger was looking at her too. She looked puzzled, as if she had always been sure what kind of person Draconia was, and was not so sure any more. Draconia lifted her chin and looked at a tree.

✦ ✦ ✦

She did not sleep. She sat up in bed with her curtains drawn and a sheet of the thick cream writing paper on her knee. She told her father everything. She always had.

Dear Father. Tonight I was sent into the Forbidden Forest like a servant. There was a dead unicorn, and a

She stopped.

To tell it properly, she would have to write that she had run away. He would be ashamed of that. Then she would have to write that she had gone back, for Harry Potter, and screamed for the gamekeeper to save them. He would be even more ashamed of that.

There was no way to tell the truth that would please him.

So she did not send it. She folded the letter up very small and pushed it to the bottom of her trunk. It was the first thing she had ever kept from him.

At breakfast she took her usual place at the end of the Slytherin table, nearest the doors. She had given up saving a seat for Potter months ago. Pansy had it now. But she had never moved from that end of the table, because from there she could see the whole of the Gryffindor one.

He came in at ten past eight. He was alive. His hair looked worse than ever. Nobody at his table spoke to him, and he sat down at the very end of it, alone.

Then he looked across the Hall. Past the Ravenclaws. Past the Hufflepuffs. All the way to her.

And he nodded. Just once.

She understood it. It meant: I know what you did. You came back.

Nobody had ever nodded at her like that. People nodded at her because of who her father was. This one was for something she had done herself.

She had no idea what to do with it. So she did the thing she always did. On her way out she walked the long way round, behind his bench, and said, "You've got leaves in your hair, Potter."

His hand went up. "No, I haven't."

"Well, it looks as though you have. It always does."

And there it was. Not the cold frown from the Forest. The small, annoyed one she liked, the one she had been missing for weeks.

Hello, thought Draconia. It was the best she had felt in twelve hours.

On the way to her first lesson she made herself a promise. She was never going into that Forest again. Not for a detention, not for a dragon, not for anybody.

She kept that promise for nearly six years.

When she finally broke it, she walked in alone. And that time, she did not scream.

Chapter 2.3: The House Cup

Year One. June. The last week of term.

Harry Potter had been unconscious for two days, and nobody would tell Draconia why.

What had happened was supposed to be a secret. So, naturally, the whole school knew about it. The trouble was that the whole school knew six different versions.

In one version, Potter, Weasley and Granger had gone through a trapdoor on the third floor, past a dog with three heads. In another, something valuable had been hidden under the castle, and a teacher had tried to steal it. Everybody agreed about the teacher. It was Professor Quirrell, the nervous one with the turban, and he was gone. Nobody knew where.

And everybody agreed about the ending. Harry Potter had gone down there to stop him. He had been carried out again, and he had not woken up since.

Draconia listened to all six versions at breakfast and did not eat anything.

She kept thinking about the Forest. She kept thinking about the hooded thing that had drunk the unicorn's blood, and how it had got up and gone straight towards him. Nobody had ever told her what it was. She had a feeling that it had found him again.

She did not say so. She had not told anyone about the Forest at all.

✦ ✦ ✦

The hospital wing was on the first floor. It was not on the way to anywhere Draconia needed to go.

She walked past it anyway. Then she walked past it again.

The third time, the door was open a crack. She could see the end of one bed. There was a table beside it, piled so high with sweets that some of them had fallen on the floor. Half the school seemed to have sent him something. Above the pile she could see a tuft of untidy black hair on a pillow. It was not moving.

She stood there with her hand in her pocket.

There was a Chocolate Frog card in the pocket. It was the one from the train, on the very first day, when even the frog had jumped out of the window to get away from her. She had kept it all year without quite knowing why.

She could go in. She could put it on the table with the rest. Nobody would know which one was hers.

But she would know. A Malfoy did not visit people. People visited a Malfoy.

"That's three times," said a voice behind her.

Pansy Parkinson was leaning against the wall with her arms folded.

"I am taking the air," said Draconia.

"Indoors?"

"It's a very airy corridor."

Pansy looked at the door, and then at Draconia's face. She did not laugh. A year ago Draconia would have expected her to.

"He's going to be all right," Pansy said. "Madam Pomfrey told a Hufflepuff, and the Hufflepuff told Millicent. He's only sleeping. He'll be up in time for the feast."

Madam Pomfrey was the school nurse. She did not say things she didn't mean.

Draconia let out a breath. She had not known she was holding it.

"I wasn't worried," she said.

"No," Pansy agreed. "You were taking the air. Come on. You've missed lunch."

Draconia went with her. The card stayed in her pocket.

She was not a person who went in. Not yet.

✦ ✦ ✦

The end-of-year feast was the best evening of Draconia's life, for about twenty minutes.

All year long the four houses competed for points. You won them for good work and lost them for breaking rules. At the end of the year, the house with the most points won the House Cup.

Slytherin had won it six years in a row. Tonight made seven.

The whole Great Hall was hung in green and silver. A huge banner with the Slytherin serpent on it covered the wall behind the teachers. Draconia sat under it at the Slytherin table and felt as tall as the ceiling.

She had helped. The hundred and fifty points Gryffindor lost in May had put them at the very bottom. That had been her. She had done it from the foot of a staircase, in her dressing gown.

The doors opened, and the Hall went quiet. Harry Potter walked in.

He was pale, and he looked thinner. But he was on his feet. He sat down between Weasley and Granger, and the whole Hall began whispering at once.

Draconia looked at her plate. Something in her chest that had been tight for three days came loose.

Then the Headmaster stood up.

Professor Dumbledore was very old, with a silver beard down to his belt. He read out the points. Gryffindor was last, with three hundred and twelve. Slytherin was first, with four hundred and seventy-two. The Slytherin table roared, and Draconia banged her goblet with the rest.

"Yes, yes, well done, Slytherin," said Dumbledore. "However, recent events must be taken into account."

The Slytherin table stopped roaring.

"I have a few last-minute points to give out," he said.

He gave Ron Weasley fifty points, for the best game of chess the school had seen in years. He gave Hermione Granger fifty points, for staying calm and thinking clearly when it mattered. Then he gave Harry Potter sixty points, for nerve and courage.

Draconia was counting. She could not help it. It was her father's habit.

Three hundred and twelve, and a hundred and sixty. Four hundred and seventy-two.

A tie.

She was gripping her goblet so hard that her fingers hurt. It was the Seeker place all over again. The rules were being bent for Harry Potter, in front of everyone, and nobody had asked her what she thought.

"There are all kinds of courage," said Dumbledore. "It takes a great deal of bravery to stand up to our enemies. It takes just as much to stand up to our friends. I therefore award ten points to Mr. Neville Longbottom."

The Gryffindor table exploded.

Dumbledore clapped his hands. The green and silver turned to red and gold. The great serpent on the wall melted away, and a lion stood in its place.

All along the Slytherin table, people sat as if they had been turned to stone. Marcus Flint had gone purple. At the teachers' table, Professor Snape, the head of Slytherin, was shaking Professor McGonagall's hand with a smile that looked painful.

Draconia waited to be furious.

She was furious about the first hundred and sixty. That was simple.

It was the last ten that she could not get past.

To stand up to our friends. Longbottom had done that. He had told his own friends they were wrong, to their faces, knowing they would be angry.

She thought about Vivienne, who could not tell her she was wrong about a ghost. She thought about Greta saying nobody ever asked me where I wanted to sit. She thought about Pansy saying no at Halloween.

And she thought about herself. She had never once stood up to anyone she cared about. She had never told her father he was wrong about anything. She had not even sent him a letter.

Neville Longbottom was the boy she had scared in the Forest for fun. He had just done something she had never been brave enough to do.

She could not call those ten points unfair. She tried, and she couldn't.

"You're taking this very well," said Pansy quietly.

"I am furious."

"You're not, though. Not as much as you should be." Pansy tilted her head. "Flint looks as though he's going to be sick. You just look as though you're thinking."

Draconia did not answer that.

Across the Hall, the Gryffindors were hugging Longbottom, who had disappeared under a pile of them. In the middle of the noise, Harry Potter looked up. He looked past the Ravenclaws and the Hufflepuffs, all the way to her.

He put one hand up to his hair and patted it, slowly, as if he was checking it for something. Then he looked at his empty hand, and shrugged.

No leaves.

It was a joke. It was a joke that only the two of them in the whole Hall could understand.

Draconia felt her mouth start to smile. She turned it into a sneer as fast as she could. It was not fast enough.

"What was that?" said Pansy.

"Nothing."

"He did something with his hair, and you went pink."

"It's very warm in here," said Draconia. "There are a great many candles."

✦ ✦ ✦

The exam results came out the next morning.

Hermione Granger was first in the whole year. Draconia Malfoy was second, by four marks.

She read the list twice. She had expected to be first. She had always been first, at home, where there was nobody else.

At home she would have said that the Granger girl must have cheated. She found that she could not say it here. She had watched Granger work. Granger had not cheated. Granger had simply read everything three times.

"Four marks," Draconia said. "Four."

"That's good, isn't it?" said Greta.

"It is not first."

She folded the list up very small. Next year she would read everything four times.

✦ ✦ ✦

The train left the next morning.

She had the front compartment again, the best one. Vivienne and Greta lifted her trunk. That was the same as September. But this time Pansy sat down next to her without asking, and talked the whole way to the first station, and Draconia let her.

Halfway through the journey she went for a walk.

Third carriage from the back. The door was half open. There were sweet wrappers all over the floor, just like last time. Weasley was there, and Granger, and Potter, in the seat by the window.

In September she had stood in this exact doorway and held out her hand to him. He had looked at it and said no.

She had been sure, back then, that he would be sorry within a week. He would come and ask to sit with her. She had saved him a seat for two months.

He had never come. She had lost the House Cup to him. She had come second to his friend. She had run away from a monster in front of him, and gone back.

And she was standing in his doorway again.

"Potter," she said.

All three of them looked up. Weasley scowled.

"Try not to do anything stupid over the summer," said Draconia. "It would be very dull next year without somebody to beat."

"You didn't beat me at anything," said Harry.

"I'm going to. I've asked my father for a broom."

"Okay." He almost smiled. "See you next year, Malfoy."

"Don't count on it, Four Eyes."

"It's still Harry."

"I know what your name is."

She had said that to him once before, right here, and stormed off. This time she walked away at a normal speed.

She knew what his name was. She had tried saying it once, in her head, on the first night, and it had felt like losing. She tried it again now, walking up the train.

Harry.

It still felt like losing. But it did not feel as bad as it had in September.

Behind her she heard Weasley say, "Is she always like that?"

"Yeah," said Harry. "Pretty much."

He did not sound as if he minded.

✦ ✦ ✦

Her parents were waiting on the platform in London.

Her mother kissed her on both cheeks and said she had got thin. Her father tipped her chin up with one gloved finger and studied her face, the way he studied the family silver. He was checking for tarnish.

Vivienne's father and Greta's father were standing a little way behind him. They both nodded to him before they even looked at their own daughters.

"Well," said Lucius Malfoy. "Did you make the right friends?"

A year ago she would have said yes, Father.

"I made one," said Draconia. "I chose her myself."

Further down the platform, Pansy was being pulled away by her mother, waving with her whole arm and still talking.

Her father raised one eyebrow. He let it pass.

"And the Potter boy? You mentioned him in your letters. More than once."

She looked along the platform. She did not mean to.

Harry Potter was dragging his trunk towards a large man with a purple face and no neck. The man did not smile. He did not take the trunk. He just turned round and walked off, and Potter followed him.

Draconia's mother still had a hand on her shoulder.

She remembered the white owl that had come to his table every morning with nothing to give him. She had thought his family must be very busy. She understood now that she had been wrong about that. Nobody at that boy's home was glad he was coming back.

It had never occurred to her that going home could be something a person did not look forward to.

"He's impossible," she said. "And his hair is a disgrace."

"Is it," said her father.

It was the first time she described Harry Potter's hair to him that summer. There would be ten more. Her father counted.

She did not tell him about the Forest. The letter she had never sent was still at the bottom of her trunk.

She had gone to school in September with nothing to hide, and with nobody who had ever told her no.

She came home with three things. She had a friend she had picked for herself. She had a secret she was keeping from her father. And she had a boy she could not stop looking at, who had said no to her and had been right.

She did not know yet what any of them was going to cost her.

But she held her chin up all the way to the Apparition point.. That part had not changed.

Chapter Three: The Catch

Borgin and Burkes was the most boring shop in the entire world, and Draconia had been standing in it for twenty minutes.

It was dim and dusty and smelled of old candle smoke. The shelves held a withered hand on a cushion, a silver hand mirror with a card propped against it that read DO NOT LOOK INTO. PREVIOUS OWNER STILL INSIDE, and a tall black cabinet in the corner that looked like a coffin that had given up on being subtle.

Her father was at the counter, speaking to Mr. Borgin in the low, clipped voice he used for business he didn't want written down. Something about the Ministry. Something about inspections. Draconia wasn't listening.

She was talking about Harry Potter.

"...and he doesn't even deserve it," she was saying. "He got onto the team in his first year, Father, which is against the rules, and the Gryffindors all carry on as if he invented flying. And now he gets his picture in the paper for everything. Harry Potter and his scar. Harry Potter and his broomstick. Harry Potter and his stupid, ridiculous, completely unbrushed hair..."

"Draconia."

"...and he doesn't even know anything, he didn't even know what Quidditch was, and he still..."

"Draconia." Her father did not turn around. "You have now described Mr. Potter's hair to me on eleven separate occasions this summer. I have been counting."

She closed her mouth.

"It's very bad hair," she said, with dignity.

"I'm sure." Lucius signed something with a long black quill. "You'll be pleased to know I've dealt with the matter of the broom. The Slytherin team will receive seven Nimbus Two Thousand and Ones this term. A gift from the family." He paused. "And in return, Marcus Flint has discovered that he needs a new Seeker."

Draconia forgot about Harry's hair entirely.

"Truly?"

"Truly." He glanced down at her, and for just a moment the corner of his mouth moved. "I told you. We'll see means yes."

She wanted to throw her arms around him. Malfoys didn't do that in shops. So instead she clasped her hands behind her back very tightly and bounced once, very slightly, on her toes, and then drifted toward the corner to hide her face from Mr. Borgin.

The tall black cabinet loomed over her. She reached out to trace the carved wood of its door.

Inside, something went thunk.

She snatched her hand back.

"Don't touch anything," said her father, without looking. "Half of it bites."

Draconia backed away from the cabinet, eyeing it. Whatever was in there, it was probably cursed.

She had no way of knowing it was wearing glasses.

✦ ✦ ✦

She found out at Flourish and Blotts.

The bookshop was packed wall to wall with witches waiting for Gilderoy Lockhart, who was signing his autobiography with a peacock-feather quill and a smile so bright it was practically a spell. Draconia had come to see how many copies Lockhart would sell before somebody noticed his books were ridiculous.

Instead, she saw Harry Potter get dragged out of the crowd by the famous man himself and photographed for the front page of the Daily Prophet, red-faced and blinking, with Lockhart's arm around his shoulders.

It was perfect. She couldn't have planned it better.

She waited until he'd escaped, clutching an armful of free books, and then she stepped neatly into his path at the bottom of the stairs.

"Enjoying yourself, Potter? Famous Harry Potter," she drawled. "Can't even go into a bookshop without making the front page."

Harry looked at her.

He didn't frown. That was her first warning. Instead, something very slow and very pleased spread across his face.

"Better than a scar and a broomstick and stupid, ridiculous, completely unbrushed hair," he said. "Is it?"

Draconia froze.

The noise of the bookshop seemed to fall away. There was a peculiar ringing in her ears.

"How," she said, "do you..."

"Borgin and Burkes." Harry shifted his books to one arm. "I got lost. Took the wrong fireplace. Ended up hiding in a cabinet." He tilted his head. "Eleven times, your dad said. I thought that was a lot."

Every drop of blood in Draconia's body rushed to her face at once. She was fairly sure her eyebrows had gone red.

"I..." she said. "That was... I was complaining..."

"Yeah," said Harry. "About my hair. Eleven times." He studied her with polite interest. "What's wrong with it, exactly?"

"Everything!"

A little red-haired girl behind Harry, the youngest Weasley, made a small choking noise and hid behind her mother.

Draconia turned on her heel and walked straight out of Flourish and Blotts, into the bright August sunshine, where she stood very still beside a lamppost and considered, seriously, moving to Durmstrang.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Slytherin team had the pitch booked for Saturday morning practice, and so did the Gryffindors.

The Slytherins had a note from Professor Snape. The Gryffindors, it turned out, did not.

"Oh dear," said Marcus Flint, in the green-and-silver crowd on the grass, sounding very pleased. "Looks like you'll have to come back later, Wood."

Draconia stood at the front of the Slytherin team in her new robes, with her new broom, feeling taller than she had ever felt in her life. The Nimbus Two Thousand and One was sleek and black with a silver finish that caught the morning light, and every single Gryffindor on the pitch was staring at it.

Including the Gryffindor Seeker.

"You're the new Seeker?" Harry said.

"Obviously." She held the broom up so that he could see the gold lettering on the handle. "Brand new. The fastest broom in the world. Seven of them, actually. My father bought the whole team a set."

"So you bought your way on," said a voice behind Harry.

Hermione Granger had come down to watch, with Ron Weasley at her side. She was holding a book against her chest like a shield, and her chin was up.

"Funny," Hermione said. "Nobody on our team needed a shopping trip to get picked."

A few of the Slytherins laughed. Not at Hermione. At Draconia.

And Draconia, who had been told not you by an entire school last year and had stood there and taken it, felt something hot and ugly surge up in her throat.

Her father had a word for people like Hermione Granger. He used it at dinner. He used it at parties, lightly, the way you might mention the weather, and everybody at the table would smile. Draconia had heard it a hundred times.

She'd just never said it herself.

"Nobody was talking to you," she said, "you little Mudblood."

For a heartbeat, nothing happened.

Then everything happened at once. Fred and George Weasley lunged forward and had to be held back by their captain. Ron Weasley whipped out his wand, which had been broken and mended with Spellotape, and bellowed a curse at her, and the wand backfired with a flash and a bang and knocked him flat on the grass.

He sat up, opened his mouth, and a slug fell out.

The Slytherins shrieked with laughter. Draconia laughed too.

It came out too high.

"What does it mean?" said Hermione.

She was kneeling beside Ron, holding his shoulders while he retched. She was looking up at Draconia with an odd, puzzled face. Not hurt. Not yet. Just confused. She'd grown up with Muggles. She had no idea.

"What does it mean?" she said again. "Mudblood?"

And Draconia opened her mouth to tell her, and found that she couldn't.

She had heard the word a hundred times. She knew exactly what it meant: dirty. Worthless. Less than us. She knew how it sounded in her father's voice, smooth and pleasant, across a candlelit table.

She had never heard how it sounded in her own voice, out loud, on a bright morning, said to a girl kneeling in the grass who didn't even know enough to be hurt by it yet.

It sounded like something she'd stepped in.

"Ask your friends," she said finally, and turned away.

That was when she saw Harry's face.

She had braced herself for the crease. She'd have welcomed it. She'd have enjoyed it.

It wasn't there.

He was just looking at her. Not angry. Not frowning. His face had gone quiet and closed, the way it had in Madam Malkin's when she'd asked about his parents, and he was looking at her the way you look at something you've just realized is exactly what everyone warned you it was.

It was the worst look anyone had ever given her.

He turned his back, and helped Hermione lift Ron to his feet, and the three of them walked away toward Hagrid's hut together without looking back.

Draconia stood in the middle of the pitch with her brand new broom, the fastest in the world, and felt about two inches tall.

It's just a word, she told herself. Father says it all the time. It's just a word.

She didn't say it again for a very long time.

✦ ✦ ✦

The first match of the season was Slytherin against Gryffindor, and it was pouring with rain.

Thunder rolled over the mountains. The stands were a blur of umbrellas and wet scarves, and the wind was so strong that Draconia's hair had come loose from its ribbon before she'd even left the ground. She didn't care. She was on her Nimbus, and she was in the air, and she was Seeker, and in the Gryffindor colors across the pitch was the boy she was going to beat in front of the entire school.

He hadn't spoken to her since the morning on the pitch. He hadn't even looked at her. She told herself that was a relief.

Madam Hooch blew the whistle. Fourteen players shot into the sky.

And from the very first minute, something was wrong.

One of the Bludgers, the heavy iron balls that were supposed to fly around the pitch attacking whoever was nearest, wasn't attacking whoever was nearest. It was attacking Harry. Only Harry. It came at him again and again, ignoring every other player, swerving around the Beaters who swung at it, doubling back whenever he dodged.

"Having trouble, Potter?" Draconia called, as he corkscrewed wildly past her with the Bludger roaring after him. "Is that dancing lessons or flying?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have time. The Bludger came at his head, and he dropped like a stone, and it missed him by an inch.

Her laugh died in her throat.

That's not right, she thought. Bludgers don't do that.

She drifted higher, scanning the rain for the tiny gold flicker of the Snitch, and kept losing her concentration, because every time she looked away from the sky she found herself looking at Harry instead. At the Bludger. At how close it kept coming.

It's going to hit him.

So? Good. Serves him right.

It's going to break his neck.

She clenched her hands on her broom handle until the wood bit her palms, exactly like the day of the Remembrall, and hated herself for it.

Then she saw the Snitch.

It was hovering in the rain below her, maybe forty feet from the grass, a tiny beating flicker of gold. Nobody else had seen it. Harry was on the other side of the pitch, still dodging.

Draconia dived.

The Nimbus was faster than anything she had ever flown. The wind screamed past her ears and the rain stung her face, and the Snitch grew larger and larger: the size of a walnut, the size of a plum, close enough to see its silver wings blurring. Her hand was out. Her fingers were stretching.

Mine, she thought. This one is mine.

Then there was a streak of scarlet beside her. Harry had seen her dive and followed, and he was level with her now, leaning flat along his broom, his hand stretching out beside hers.

And the rogue Bludger, still chasing him, came roaring down out of the rain behind them both.

It missed Harry by an inch.

It didn't miss her broom.

There was a sickening crack. The Nimbus bucked beneath her like a living thing, and spun, and the handle wrenched out of her wet hands, and then there was nothing under Draconia Malfoy at all.

She was falling.

The world turned over. Rain and sky and grass and rain again. She heard the whole stadium scream, a long rising roar of it, and she thought, very clearly and very calmly: Forty feet.

Then something hit her.

Not the ground. The ground was not warm. The ground did not have an arm that locked around her back and another that swept up beneath her knees and caught her, hard, against a chest that smelled of rain and broom polish. The ground did not jolt, and swerve, and then level out and drift, very slowly, the last few feet down onto the soaking grass.

She opened her eyes.

Harry Potter was holding her.

Not steadying her. Not catching her wrist. Holding her, one arm around her back and one under her knees, the way people were carried in her mother's storybooks. His glasses were streaked with rain. His hair was plastered flat for once. He was breathing hard, and his face was very, very white, and very, very close.

The Snitch had been right in front of him. She'd seen it. He'd been an inch away.

He'd let it go.

He let it go for me.

"You all right?" said Harry.

It was exactly what he'd said on the platform at Hogsmeade, a year ago, in the rain. The whole stadium had gone silent. Draconia could feel hundreds of eyes on them, and her cheeks burned so hot she was surprised the rain didn't steam off them.

She said the only thing she could think of.

"Unhand me."

Harry stared at her.

"Again?" he said.

Then the Bludger came back.

She saw it over his shoulder, a black blur hurtling out of the rain straight at them, and she didn't even have time to shout. Harry saw her face and twisted, turning his back to the Bludger, putting himself between it and her.

It hit his arm.

She heard it break. She would remember that sound for the rest of her life.

Harry made a noise through his teeth and staggered, and somehow didn't drop her, and set her down on the grass with his one good arm as the Beaters finally tackled the Bludger and pinned it to the ground.

And then he went very still, staring past her ear.

"Don't move," he said.

"What?"

"Don't move."

He reached past her face with his left hand, so close she felt his wet sleeve brush her cheek, and closed his fingers around something that had been fluttering beside her hair.

When he pulled his hand back, the Snitch was beating its wings against his fist.

It had been hovering beside her ear the entire time.

The whistle went. The Gryffindor stands exploded. And Harry Potter, the boy who had let the Snitch go to catch her, sat down very suddenly on the grass with his arm bent in a direction arms were not supposed to go, and fainted.

✦ ✦ ✦

He was in the hospital wing for two nights. Professor Lockhart had tried to mend his arm on the pitch and had somehow removed all the bones in it instead, and Madam Pomfrey had to grow them back overnight, which was, by all accounts, extremely painful.

Draconia knew this because she had asked. Casually. Three different people.

The Slytherin common room had talked about nothing else all evening. Potter chose Malfoy over the Snitch. Potter caught Malfoy like a bride. Did you see her face? Did you see his? Pansy had followed her around from the fire to the stairs to her bed, asking whether it had been romantic, until Draconia had put a Silencing Charm on her bed curtains and lain there in the green dark with her face in her pillow.

At midnight, she got up.

She told herself she was only going to the kitchens. She told herself she'd happened to bring the box of Honeydukes Sugar Quills that her mother had sent that week. She told herself it was a very long way from the dungeons to the hospital wing, and that she was only walking there because she had nothing better to do.

The hospital wing was dark except for a single lamp. Harry was asleep in the end bed, his right arm bandaged and propped on a pillow, his glasses folded on the bedside table.

He looked younger asleep. He looked like he'd never had a Bludger aimed at his head in his life.

She put the box of Sugar Quills on the table beside his glasses. She didn't leave a note.

Then she stood there for a moment longer than she needed to.

"You're an idiot, Potter," she whispered. "You had it. You were an inch away."

He didn't wake.

"Nobody asked you to catch me."

The lamp flickered.

"...Thank you," said Draconia Malfoy, very quietly, to someone who couldn't hear it. It was the first time in her life she'd ever said those words and meant them, and it was so much harder than please that she had to leave immediately.

✦ ✦ ✦

He was back at breakfast on Tuesday, with his arm in a sling and a Sugar Quill tucked behind his ear like a pencil.

Draconia stared at it across the Great Hall.

He looked up. He saw her staring. And he took the Sugar Quill from behind his ear and put it in his mouth, very deliberately, and raised his eyebrows at her.

She looked down at her plate.

It doesn't prove anything, she thought. Anyone could have sent those. Anyone at all.

It wasn't until Pansy leaned over and whispered, "Isn't that your mother's Honeydukes box? With the family crest on the lid?" that Draconia realized she was going to have to transfer to Durmstrang after all.

Chapter Four: Scared, Potter?

The message appeared on Halloween.

Draconia was coming up from the feast with the rest of the Slytherins, full of pumpkin tart and bored of Pansy, when the crowd in front of her stopped so suddenly that she walked into Greta's back.

The corridor on the second floor was flooded with water. Torchlight shivered on the wet stone. And on the wall, between two windows, someone had painted letters a foot high in something dark and gleaming. The words said that the Chamber of Secrets had been opened, and that the enemies of the Heir should be afraid.

Below them, hanging stiff as a board from a torch bracket by her tail, was Mrs. Norris, the caretaker's cat.

Her eyes were open. She wasn't moving.

And standing in front of the wall, in the middle of the water, pale and horrified, were Harry Potter, Ron Weasley, and Hermione Granger.

The Slytherins around her started to laugh. Somebody at the back shouted something about Muggle-borns being next, and more of them laughed louder, and Draconia opened her mouth to join in, because that was what you did.

Nothing came out.

She was looking at the painted letters. At enemies. And she was remembering a bright morning on the Quidditch pitch, and a girl kneeling in the grass asking what a word meant.

The Chamber of Secrets. Every pure-blood child knew the story. Salazar Slytherin had built a hidden chamber in the castle, and left a monster in it, and one day his true heir would come back and open it and purge the school of everyone unworthy.

It had always been a bedtime story. The kind her father told with a little smile.

It didn't feel like a bedtime story now, with the water soaking through her shoes and a cat hanging frozen on the wall.

Across the corridor, Harry looked up. His eyes met hers over the heads of the crowd.

She waited for the accusation in them. You. Your family. Your house.

It wasn't there. He only looked tired, and scared, and very young.

Then Filch came around the corner and started screaming, and the teachers arrived, and she lost sight of him in the crush.

✦ ✦ ✦

By December, half the school was sure Harry Potter was the Heir of Slytherin.

It had started with the cat, because he'd been found standing over it. Then a Gryffindor first-year called Colin Creevey was found petrified at the foot of a staircase, still clutching his camera, and people began edging away from Harry in the corridors.

And then came the Dueling Club.

Professor Lockhart set it up in the Great Hall, with the house tables cleared away and a long golden stage down the middle. He had apparently decided that what a school under attack from an ancient monster needed was a lesson in how to bow.

Professor Snape was his assistant. Snape looked like a man who had been asked to assist a peacock with its taxes.

"Pair up!" Lockhart cried, after he'd been blasted off his feet by Snape's very first spell and pretended he'd meant it. "Everyone find a partner!"

Snape swept down the line of students, sorting them with brisk flicks of his wand. He reached Harry and paused, lip curling.

"Potter," he said silkily. "I think perhaps..."

"I'll take him, sir."

Draconia had already stepped forward.

Snape looked at her. Something that might have been amusement flickered across his sallow face. "By all means, Miss Malfoy."

She climbed onto the stage and walked to the center, and Harry climbed up after her, and they faced each other with their wands raised like Lockhart had shown them. The whole hall seemed to tilt toward them. People had heard about the Quidditch match. People had heard about a lot of things.

She smiled at him.

"Scared, Potter?" she said.

Say something, she thought. Frown at me. Go on. I haven't seen it in weeks.

He studied her across the six feet of golden stage.

"Of you?" said Harry. "Not today."

And there it was: not the crease, but the ghost of that crooked private smile from the Great Hall a year ago. Her stomach flipped over. She told herself it was nerves.

"On the count of three," Lockhart trilled. "Bow to your partners! One... two..."

Draconia didn't wait for three. She never did.

"Rictusempra!" said Harry, a second later, and a jet of silver light hit her in the stomach, and she doubled up wheezing, and not because it tickled.

It did tickle. It was humiliating. She staggered, clutching her ribs, giggling helplessly while the Gryffindors howled, and she was so furious that she didn't think. She pointed her wand at the floor between them and said the first thing her father had ever taught her that looked impressive.

"Serpensortia!"

The end of her wand exploded.

A long black snake shot out of it and fell heavily onto the stage between them, and raised itself up, hissing, ready to strike.

The hall went dead silent.

Draconia's giggles stopped. She had never actually tried the spell before. She'd only heard her father describe it. She had not expected the snake to be quite so large.

"Don't move, Miss Malfoy," said Snape lazily, clearly enjoying himself. "I'll get rid of it..."

"Allow me!" cried Lockhart, and flourished his wand at the snake, and there was a loud bang, and instead of vanishing, the snake flew ten feet into the air and landed with a smack on the floor again, furious. It swung its head toward the nearest person, a Hufflepuff called Justin Finch-Fletchley, and bared its fangs.

And Harry walked toward it.

He didn't draw his wand. He just walked, as if something was pulling him, and opened his mouth, and a sound came out of it that made every hair on Draconia's arms stand up.

It wasn't English. It wasn't any language she'd ever heard. It was a long, low, rasping hiss, the sound the snake was making, coming out of a boy's mouth.

The snake stopped.

Slowly, it lowered its head. It curled up on the floor like a coil of thick black rope and lay still, gazing up at Harry.

Draconia stared at him.

Parseltongue.

The rarest gift in the wizarding world. The language of serpents. The mark of Salazar Slytherin himself, passed down through his blood. Her father spoke of it in a hushed voice, the way other people spoke of royalty.

And Harry Potter, who had turned down her hand on the train, who had been sorted into Gryffindor while she held her breath, who had refused Slytherin as if it were a disease, spoke the language of Slytherin.

He would have been ours, she thought, dizzy. The Hat must have known. He should have been ours.

Then Snape vanished the snake, and the spell broke, and the whole hall was muttering and backing away from Harry, and Ron Weasley was grabbing his arm and hauling him out through the doors.

He looked back once, from the doorway.

Not at Justin. Not at the teachers. At her.

His face said, plainly: What just happened?

She didn't have an answer. For the first time since she'd met him, she didn't have anything at all.

✦ ✦ ✦

"It's obviously him," said Pansy that night, in the Slytherin common room. "A Parselmouth. He's the Heir. He's been setting it on Muggle-borns all along."

"Potter?" Draconia didn't look up from her Transfiguration essay. "Don't be ridiculous."

"But he talked to it! Everyone saw!"

"And?" Draconia dipped her quill. "He talked to it to make it stop. He walked straight up to it in front of three hundred people. He's a Gryffindor to his bones, Pansy. If he wanted to hurt someone, he wouldn't set a secret monster on them from the shadows. He'd walk right up to them and punch them in the face." She crossed out a word. "He'd rather eat his own broom than sneak."

The common room had gone rather quiet.

She looked up. Pansy was staring at her. So were Millicent and two fourth-years by the fire.

"What?" said Draconia.

"Nothing," said Pansy, very slowly. "You just seem to know a lot about how Potter would punch someone."

Draconia went back to her essay.

"I'm observant," she said.

✦ ✦ ✦

The school emptied for Christmas. Her parents were travelling, so Draconia stayed, which suited her perfectly. The castle was quiet and the Slytherin table was nearly empty, and on Christmas Day she ate so much trifle that she felt faintly ill.

She was on her way back to the dungeons afterward when she found Vivienne and Greta wandering in the entrance hall, looking lost.

That was unusual in itself. Vivienne and Greta were never lost. They were always exactly one step behind her. But these two were standing by the marble staircase staring around them like first-years, and when they saw her, they both jumped.

"There you are," said Draconia. "Where have you been? You missed pudding. You never miss pudding."

"We were..." said Vivienne, in a voice that was oddly deep for her. She cleared her throat. "Library."

Draconia stared at her.

"Vivienne," she said, "you have never been in the library in your life."

Greta made a small choking noise. Vivienne's eyes, which were Vivienne's small dark eyes but somehow didn't look like them, darted sideways.

"...Studying," Vivienne said, weakly.

Draconia decided the trifle must have gone to their heads. "Come on," she said, and led the way down to the dungeons.

It was strange. Vivienne walked beside her all the way, and every so often Draconia caught her sneaking looks at her, sideways, as if she'd never seen her before. At the bare stretch of wall that hid the common room, Draconia said the password, and Vivienne actually flinched when the stone door slid open.

"Honestly," said Draconia. "What's wrong with you two today?"

"Stomachache," said Greta instantly.

The common room was empty except for them. Draconia flopped into her favorite high-backed chair by the fire, and Vivienne and Greta perched on the sofa opposite, very stiffly, as though it might bite them.

She looked at the flames for a while.

It was easier, somehow, with the castle so empty. With nobody else here. Vivienne and Greta never said anything. You could tell them things and they'd just nod. They were like talking to the furniture, only warmer.

"Do you think it's Potter?" she said abruptly. "The Heir. Everyone thinks it's Potter."

Vivienne's head jerked up. "Do you?"

"Of course not." Draconia pulled her knees up to her chin and twisted one of her gold earrings round and round. "I told Pansy. It's ridiculous. I've watched him for a year and a half. He hasn't got one sneaky bone in his entire body. He can't even lie properly. He goes red at the ears."

Vivienne was staring at her.

"I wish I knew who it was," Draconia went on, to the fire. "Father won't tell me anything. He only says it's none of my business and I'm to keep my head down. But he knows something, I can tell. He's been... pleased. All term." She frowned. "It didn't used to bother me. All that about Muggle-borns. It was just a thing people said at dinner."

"And now?" said Vivienne quietly.

Draconia was silent for a long moment.

"Granger asked me what it meant," she said. "That word. On the pitch. You were there. She didn't even know." She wrapped her arms tighter around her knees. "I don't want her to get petrified. Isn't that stupid? I can't stand her. She's a know-it-all and her hair's worse than Potter's. But I keep thinking about that cat hanging on the wall, and I keep thinking it could be her next, and it'd be..."

She stopped.

"It'd be what?" said Vivienne.

My fault, thought Draconia. A little bit. Because I said it.

"Nothing," she said. "Forget it."

The fire crackled. Greta was gripping the sofa arm very hard.

"He caught me," Draconia said suddenly, very quietly. "At the match. You saw. He let the Snitch go. Nobody's ever... Father would have gone for the Snitch. I would have gone for the Snitch." She glanced up. "Don't you dare tell anyone I said that. Either of you. I'll hex you both bald."

Vivienne didn't answer. She was looking at Draconia with the oddest expression, as though someone had just handed her something heavy and she wasn't sure where to put it down.

And then Draconia noticed something.

"Vivienne," she said, sitting up. "What's wrong with your hair?"

Vivienne's hair, which was mousy brown and always scraped back in a tight bun, was going dark. It was spreading from the roots, turning black, and at the same time it was getting... shorter? Messier? It was starting, very slightly, to stick up at the back.

And her eyes...

"Your eyes look green," said Draconia, bewildered.

Vivienne clapped a hand over her face.

"Stomachache," said Greta loudly, whose own hair had begun to turn a very distinct shade of red. She leapt up. "Bad. Really bad. Hospital wing. Now."

And they both bolted for the door, and it slammed behind them, and Draconia was left alone by the fire with her mouth open.

She sat there for a long time.

"It must have been the trifle," she said at last.

Vivienne and Greta were found three hours later, fast asleep in a broom cupboard on the ground floor, with no memory whatsoever of how they'd got there. Nobody could explain it. Draconia put it down to a house-elf prank and forgot about it.

But the next morning, when she passed Harry Potter in the entrance hall, he stopped.

He looked at her for a long moment, in a way he'd never looked at her before. Not annoyed. Not wary. Almost careful.

"Happy Christmas, Malfoy," he said.

Then he walked on, and left her standing there with absolutely no idea why her heart was pounding.

✦ ✦ ✦

On the fourteenth of February, the Great Hall was covered in pink paper hearts.

Lockhart had arranged it. He'd also arranged a dozen surly dwarves dressed as cupids, with little golden wings and harps, whose job was to deliver valentines to students all day, in person, in the middle of lessons.

Draconia thought it was the most vulgar thing she had ever seen.

She thought so right up until the afternoon, when a dwarf tackled Harry Potter to the ground outside the Charms corridor.

It was magnificent. The dwarf had tried to stop him, and Harry had tried to get away, and the dwarf had grabbed his ankles and brought him down in front of about fifty people and sat on him, and then, still sitting on him, begun to sing.

It was a valentine. It was a terrible one. It rhymed Harry's eyes with a pickled toad and his hair with a blackboard, and it went on for three whole verses while Harry lay face down on the flagstones going redder and redder, and the whole corridor, Draconia included, laughed until they cried.

"Who's it from?" someone shrieked. "Who sent it?"

Draconia turned, still laughing, and saw.

At the edge of the crowd, a small red-haired girl had gone the color of a tomato. She had both hands over her face. It was the youngest Weasley. Ginny. The one who'd hidden behind her mother in Flourish and Blotts.

And she was looking at Harry the way...

The way...

Draconia's laugh dried up.

No, she thought. No. She's eleven. She's a Weasley.

She walked away before the dwarf had finished the last verse.

✦ ✦ ✦

She told herself she was going to the girls' bathroom to wash her hands. She did wash her hands. And then she stayed there, leaning on the cold stone of the sink, looking at herself in the mirror.

She had never really looked before. She'd simply known what she looked like. Everyone told her. The Black cheekbones, her mother said. Your grandmother's eyes. She was a Malfoy. That was the whole of it.

But now she looked. Really looked.

Pale. So pale she was nearly grey in the bathroom light. Her chin was too pointed. Her hair was long and golden and completely, stubbornly straight, hanging down like a curtain; it had never once in its life done anything interesting. The gold hoops her mother had given her on her eleventh birthday (a Malfoy lady always wears gold, darling) suddenly looked far too big for her face. Her nose was fine. Probably. She wasn't sure anymore what a nose was supposed to look like.

And her eyes were green.

She had always known that, of course. Bright green, like her grandmother's. She had just never noticed, until this exact moment, that they were almost the same green as...

She stopped that thought right there, turned on the tap, and splashed cold water on her face until it went away.

Ginny Weasley had freckles. A whole map of them. And red hair that looked warm even from across a corridor, like firelight. She'd looked, going pink behind her hands, like someone out of a painting.

Draconia looked at her own reflection and thought: I look like a ghost with good posture.

She'd never thought anything like that about herself in her entire life.

She did not like it one bit.

"Ridiculous," she told the mirror. "Utterly ridiculous. He's got hair like a hedge and he was just rhymed with a pickled toad."

The mirror didn't argue. That was the trouble with mirrors.

✦ ✦ ✦

That night, when Pansy was asleep, Draconia sat up in bed with her curtains drawn and a lamp burning very low, and she took out a sheet of her best cream paper.

She didn't know why. She just did.

She dipped her quill. She wrote, in her very neatest handwriting:

Your hair is still terrible.

She looked at it for a long time.

Underneath, smaller, she added:

But you fly well.

She didn't sign it. She held it up to the lamp and read it over, and her heart was going so hard she could feel it in her ears, and she thought about the dwarves, and the whole corridor laughing, and fifty people shrieking who's it from?

Then she got up, very quietly, crossed the dormitory in her bare feet, and dropped the paper into the embers of the fire.

It curled, and blackened, and was gone.

She went back to bed and lay there with her arms folded, glaring at the canopy.

"It was a terrible poem," she muttered to the dark. "I could have written a better one in my sleep."

She would not realize, until much later, that that was exactly the problem.

✦ ✦ ✦

The attacks went on until the very end of the year. Hermione Granger was found petrified in the library in May, holding a small mirror, and Draconia sat very still at breakfast when she heard and didn't eat a thing.

Then, in the last weeks of term, it was suddenly over.

Nobody told the Slytherins much. There were rumors of a girl taken into the Chamber, and of Harry going down after her, and of a sword and a giant serpent and a phoenix. Draconia believed about half of it. The half she believed was the half with Harry walking straight into the dark to save someone, because that was exactly the kind of stupid thing he'd do.

Her father came to the school in a fury on the last week of term and left in a worse one, stripped of his seat on the board of governors and missing one house-elf, and the summer that followed was very quiet at the Manor.

Hermione Granger came back to breakfast, unpetrified and bossy as ever, and was hugged by half of Gryffindor.

Draconia watched from across the Hall.

She didn't say anything. She never would, that year.

But when Hermione happened to glance over, Draconia did not look away, and did not sneer, and did not say a single word.

It wasn't an apology. Not yet.

But it was the first time she'd ever looked at Hermione Granger and not thought that word at all.

Chapter Five: Dementors

The cold came first.

The Hogwarts Express had stopped dead somewhere in the rain-dark hills, and the lamps had gone out, and Draconia was sitting in the front compartment with Pansy and Vivienne and Greta when the window began to frost over from the edges inward, like a hand closing.

"What's happening?" Pansy whispered. "Why have we stopped?"

Nobody answered. The cold crept up through the floor, through Draconia's shoes, into her bones. It wasn't the cold of winter. It was the cold of every bad thing at once.

The compartment door slid open.

Something stood in the corridor. It was tall and cloaked and hooded, and where its face should have been there was only darkness, and a long, slow, rattling breath, as if it were trying to breathe in the whole compartment.

And Draconia heard a sound.

It wasn't a scream. It wasn't her father's voice. It was a crack, wet and awful, in the pouring rain. The sound of a bone breaking in a boy's arm, the day he turned his back on a Bludger to keep it from hitting her.

She heard it again, and again, and again, louder each time, until she had her hands over her ears and her eyes screwed shut...

Then it was gone. The lights flickered back on. The window was clearing. Pansy was crying quietly into Millicent's shoulder, and Greta was holding a Chocolate Frog in both hands like a lifeline, and the thing in the corridor had gone.

"Dementor," said Vivienne, very quietly. "They're guarding the school. Because of Sirius Black."

Draconia lowered her hands. They were shaking.

Of all the things, she thought, bewildered and furious. Of all the terrible things in my whole life. That's the worst one?

Ten minutes later, the news came down the train. The Dementor had gone into Harry Potter's compartment, and Harry Potter had fallen off his seat and fainted.

"Fainted," said Pansy, who had recovered remarkably quickly. "Like a Victorian lady. Isn't that priceless?"

"Priceless," Draconia agreed.

She waited three whole minutes before she asked whether anyone knew if he was all right. Then she waited five more and asked again.

The third time, Pansy gave her a very long look.

✦ ✦ ✦

She made fun of him at breakfast anyway. She had to. The whole Slytherin table was watching.

"Oh, Potter," she called, as he came into the Great Hall. She pressed the back of her hand to her forehead and swayed dramatically against Pansy. "Feeling faint? Shall I fetch you a chair? A nice cup of tea? Smelling salts?"

The Slytherins shrieked. Harry didn't stop walking.

But he looked at her as he passed. He didn't frown. He looked tired and grey and very much like someone who had heard something terrible in the dark last night too, and didn't want to talk about it.

Draconia's laugh went flat.

I heard your arm break, she wanted to say. That's what I heard. That's what it made me hear. So we're even. So you don't have to look like that.

She didn't say anything. She ate her toast.

It tasted of nothing at all.

✦ ✦ ✦

Care of Magical Creatures was taught by Hagrid that year, which Draconia thought was a disgrace, and it was taught outside in a paddock at the edge of the Forbidden Forest, which she thought was worse.

Then Hagrid led out the hippogriffs.

They had the bodies of horses and the heads and wings of enormous eagles, and they were beautiful in a way that made her breath catch: steel grey, bronze, rust red, storm-cloud black, their feathers gleaming, their great orange eyes proud and fierce.

"Now, the firs' thing yeh gotta know abou' hippogriffs," Hagrid boomed, "is they're proud. Easy offended. Yeh always wait fer the hippogriff ter make the firs' move. Yeh walk up, yeh bow, and yeh wait. If he bows back, yeh can touch him. If he doesn't..." He winced. "Get away sharpish. Them talons hurt."

A creature that expects to be bowed to, Draconia thought. Well. At least someone here has standards.

"Right," said Hagrid. "Who wants ter go firs'?"

Nobody moved.

Except Harry.

Of course it was Harry. He climbed over the paddock fence and walked out alone to meet the grey hippogriff Hagrid called Buckbeak. He bowed. He waited, very still, while the great beast stared him down with one fierce orange eye.

And then Buckbeak bent his scaly front knees, and sank into an unmistakable bow.

The Gryffindors cheered. And then Hagrid lifted Harry onto the hippogriff's back, and Buckbeak spread his vast grey wings and flew, a lap around the paddock, around the edge of the forest, back down again with a thud, and Harry slid off pink-cheeked and grinning, and everyone cheered again.

Again.

It was always Harry. The broom leaping into his hand. The Snitch. The snake. The hippogriff. Every time, the whole world bowed to Harry Potter, and he didn't even have to ask.

Something hot and sour rose up in her.

She climbed over the fence.

"This is very easy," she announced loudly, marching straight up to Buckbeak. "If Potter can do it, it must be." She didn't bow. She was a Malfoy. Malfoys did not bow to farm animals. She tossed her golden hair back and looked the hippogriff dead in its orange eye. "You're not dangerous at all, are you? You great, ugly, overgrown..."

It happened in a flash of steel-grey talons.

She didn't even see it move. There was a blur, and a shriek (hers), and then she was on the grass with her arm clutched to her chest and red seeping through her sleeve.

"I'm dying!" she screamed. "It's killed me! Look at it, it's killed me!"

"Yer not dyin'!" Hagrid had gone white. He was already scooping her up. "Someone help me, get her out of here..."

As Hagrid carried her past the fence, Draconia looked up through her hair and tears and saw Harry.

He wasn't laughing. He wasn't worried about her either. He was watching Hagrid, and his face was tight with something like fear. Fear for Hagrid.

And then his eyes moved to her, and there it was: the crease.

But it wasn't the old crease. The one she'd liked. This one was angry. Properly angry. You did this, it said. You did this on purpose, and you're going to get him sacked.

Her arm hurt a lot less than that look did.

✦ ✦ ✦

Madam Pomfrey mended her arm in about a minute. It didn't even leave a scar.

Draconia kept the sling for three weeks.

Partly, it was because her father had written to the school governors, and the Ministry, and the Committee for the Disposal of Dangerous Creatures, and it wouldn't do to be seen with a perfectly working arm while he was doing all that.

But mostly it was because of Potions.

"I can't possibly cut my own daisy roots, Professor," she said sweetly, on the first Thursday, holding up her bandaged arm. "Not with my injury."

Snape didn't even look up. "Potter. Cut Miss Malfoy's roots for her."

Harry looked at Snape. Then he looked at her. The crease appeared, deep and furious. Then he picked up her daisy roots and began chopping them, very hard, with his knife.

Draconia settled back on her stool.

"Smaller than that, Potter," she said. "They're uneven."

He chopped harder.

"And my shrivelfig needs skinning."

"Skin your own..."

"Potter," said Snape, silkily, from across the room.

Harry skinned her shrivelfig.

It was the most wonderful hour she had spent at Hogwarts. He was right there, a foot away, at her table, doing exactly as she said, frowning ferociously the entire time. She could see the pattern of freckles on the back of his hand, and the small scar on his knuckle, and the way his fringe fell into his eyes when he bent over the chopping board. He smelled faintly of grass and broom polish.

Unfair, she thought, gazing at his profile over the top of her Potions book. Deeply, deeply unfair.

"You're staring, Malfoy," said Harry, without looking up.

She dropped her book.

"I'm supervising," she said.

✦ ✦ ✦

Defence Against the Dark Arts was taught that year by a new professor called Lupin. He had shabby, much-mended robes and grey in his hair, although he wasn't old, and he looked as though he hadn't slept properly in years.

Draconia thought he looked poor. She said so, loudly, on the first day. Then he taught the best lesson she had ever had in her life, and she found it difficult to keep saying it.

It was a joint class, Slytherin and Gryffindor together, and he didn't take them to a classroom. He took them to the staff room, where an old wardrobe was rattling and shuddering against the wall as if something inside it very badly wanted out.

"A boggart," said Professor Lupin pleasantly. "Nobody knows what a boggart looks like when it's alone in the dark. The moment it sees you, it becomes whatever you fear most."

There was a small, uneasy silence.

"The charm that defeats it is simple," he went on. "Riddikulus. But the charm isn't the hard part. The hard part is picturing your fear, and then picturing a way to make it ridiculous. Laughter is what finishes a boggart. Line up, please."

It was, Draconia had to admit, extremely entertaining. Neville Longbottom's boggart was Professor Snape, and Neville turned him into a Snape wearing a moth-eaten dress and an enormous hat with a stuffed vulture on top, and the whole room shrieked. Pansy's boggart was a mummy, which tripped over its own bandages. Ron Weasley's was a giant spider, which ended up on roller skates.

Then it was her turn.

She stepped up with her wand raised and her chin high. She wasn't worried. She had thought about it on the way down the corridor and decided her boggart would be a Dementor, or possibly Hagrid, and either way she would put it in a frock.

The shape on the floor spun, and blurred, and rose...

And became Harry Potter.

The room went completely silent.

It wasn't a monstrous Harry. It wasn't bleeding, or snarling, or dead. It was simply him: untidy hair, school robes, round glasses. He was standing half turned away from her. And as she watched, he looked at her over his shoulder with that quiet, closed, disappointed face from the morning on the Quidditch pitch, the look he'd given her after she said that word.

"You're exactly what everyone told me you were," said boggart-Harry.

Then he turned his back on her, and began to walk away.

Draconia couldn't move.

She could feel the whole class staring. She could feel, somewhere behind her in the line, the real Harry Potter staring hardest of all. Her wand hand was shaking so badly that the tip was drawing little circles in the air.

"Miss Malfoy," said Professor Lupin, very gently. "Picture something funny. Anything at all."

Funny. Funny. Her mind was completely blank.

Then, for no reason at all, she remembered the Quidditch match in second year, and Harry twisting and spinning to dodge a Bludger. Is that dancing lessons or flying, Potter?

"Riddikulus!" she shrieked.

There was a crack. Boggart-Harry stopped walking. He was suddenly wearing a stiff, frilly, bright pink ballet tutu over his school robes, and pink satin shoes with ribbons laced up his shins, and he rose onto his toes, wobbled, and spun into a helpless, flailing pirouette.

The room exploded.

Even the Gryffindors were laughing. Even Ron was laughing, doubled over and wheezing. Draconia stood there, pink to the tips of her ears, while boggart-Harry pirouetted twice more and fell over, and Lupin stepped smoothly in front of it, and it burst into wisps of smoke.

"Excellent," said Lupin. "Five points to Slytherin." He gave her a look that was a little too kind and a little too knowing, and she hated him for it. "Next, please."

✦ ✦ ✦

By dinner, the entire school knew.

"Malfoy's boggart," people whispered as she passed. "It was Potter. Walking away from her. And then she put him in a tutu."

"Obviously it was Potter," Draconia snapped, when Pansy finally dared to bring it up in the common room. "He's the most horrible thing I can think of. Look at his hair."

Pansy was quiet for a moment.

"It didn't show you his hair, though," she said. "It showed you him walking away."

Draconia opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

She went to bed early.

The next morning she walked straight into Harry at the doors of the Great Hall, literally, because she was looking at her shoes. He stepped back. They stood there.

"A tutu, Malfoy?" he said.

"You looked very fetching."

He almost smiled. Then his face changed, went careful, the way it had at Christmas the year before.

"Why was I walking away?" he asked quietly.

"Don't," said Draconia. Her voice cracked on it. "Don't ask me that, Potter."

He looked at her for a long moment.

And he didn't. He just nodded, once, and stepped aside to let her through.

It was, she thought later, the kindest thing anybody had ever done for her. She hated him for that too.

✦ ✦ ✦

Third-years were allowed to visit Hogsmeade, the little wizarding village beyond the school gates, on certain weekends, as long as they had a permission form signed at home.

Her father had signed hers without reading it. She had watched him do it over breakfast at the Manor, between two letters to the Ministry.

On Halloween, the whole third year poured down the lane to the village in a bright, cold wind, and Draconia spent the entire afternoon being dragged by Pansy from Honeydukes to Zonko's to the Three Broomsticks. She did not have a nice time at all, because she kept looking for a head of untidy black hair in every shop, and it was never there.

"Potter's not here," she said at last, in Honeydukes, as though it were a complaint about the service.

"He hasn't got a form," said Millicent, through a mouthful of Fizzing Whizbee. "His aunt and uncle wouldn't sign it. The Muggles."

Draconia put down the box of chocolates she'd been holding.

"Wouldn't?"

"That's what the Weasleys said." Millicent shrugged. "They don't like him, apparently. Never have."

Draconia stood in the middle of Honeydukes with the warm sugar smell all around her, and she thought about her father signing her form without looking, and her mother sending sweets every single week, and a boy who had to stay behind in an empty castle on Halloween because nobody at home could be bothered to write their name.

Which house were your people in? she'd asked him in Madam Malkin's, three years ago.

I don't know.

She bought the box of chocolates anyway. She didn't know why. She carried it back up to the castle in her coat pocket, and it was still there that night, when the Fat Lady's portrait was found slashed to ribbons and the whisper went round that Sirius Black was somewhere inside the castle.

✦ ✦ ✦

Dumbledore sent every student in the school to sleep in the Great Hall.

He cleared away the house tables and conjured hundreds of squashy purple sleeping bags. Prefects guarded the doors. The Head Boy strode up and down hissing at anyone who talked. The candles went out, and the enchanted ceiling glittered overhead, full of stars.

Draconia ended up at the edge of the Slytherins, which put her at the edge of the Gryffindors, which put her, it turned out when she rolled over, about two feet away from Harry Potter.

He was lying on his back with his hands behind his head, staring up at the ceiling. His glasses were off, folded on the flagstones beside him. He looked different without them. Younger. Sort of unguarded.

She should have rolled back over. She didn't.

"Can you even see anything?" she whispered. "Without those?"

He turned his head. He didn't seem surprised to find her there.

"The stars are blurry," he whispered back. "It's still nice, though."

They were both quiet for a moment. Somewhere nearby, Ron Weasley was already snoring.

"That one," said Draconia, before she could think better of it. She pointed. "The long twisty one. Going all the way across, between those two bright ones. Can you see it?"

Harry squinted. "Sort of."

"That's Draco. The dragon." She hesitated. "That's where my name comes from."

"Oh." He looked at it for a long time. "It's big."

"It's one of the biggest constellations there is," she said, with some pride.

"That figures," said Harry, and she could hear the grin in his voice even in the dark.

She should have been offended. She found she wasn't.

"You weren't in Hogsmeade today," she said, very quietly.

The grin went out of his voice. "No."

"Millicent said your relatives wouldn't sign the form."

A pause. "Yeah."

She reached into her coat, which she'd rolled up as a pillow, found the box of chocolates, and slid it across the two feet of flagstones between them without looking at him.

"These were left over," she lied. "I don't like the caramel ones."

There was a long silence. She heard the rustle of the lid.

"There aren't any caramel ones," said Harry. "They're all strawberry."

"Then I suppose I don't like strawberry either. Go to sleep, Potter."

"Silence!" hissed the Head Boy, from somewhere horribly close, and they both froze and lay very still with their eyes shut until his footsteps had gone.

She didn't sleep for a long time. She lay there looking up at the long, twisting shape of her constellation, listening to Harry Potter eating strawberry chocolates very quietly in the dark, and she felt, for the first time since she'd come to Hogwarts, completely and entirely safe.

Which was absurd, with a mass murderer loose somewhere in the castle.

She fell asleep anyway.

✦ ✦ ✦

In November, Gryffindor played Hufflepuff in the worst storm anyone could remember.

Slytherin wasn't playing, so Draconia sat in the stands in the pouring rain with Pansy, wrapped in her green scarf, and told herself she was only there to watch Potter lose.

He was so small up there. Just a scarlet dot, streaking in and out of cloud, lit silver by the lightning. She kept losing him in the rain and finding him again.

Then the cold came.

She felt it before she saw them. The rain on her face turned to ice. Her breath smoked. And then she saw them: a hundred Dementors, maybe more, standing on the pitch below, their hooded faces turned up to the sky.

Up toward one small scarlet dot.

The scarlet dot stopped. And then it fell.

It didn't dive. It didn't swerve. It just fell, straight down, limp as a dropped glove, from fifty feet, a hundred feet, turning over and over in the rain.

Draconia was on her feet.

She didn't remember standing. She was halfway down the stand steps with her scarf flying off behind her, her gold hoops cold against her neck, shoving past Slytherins, and she was screaming, and she didn't even know what she was screaming, only that it was his name. POTTER! POTTER! The storm tore it out of her mouth and threw it away.

It wouldn't have mattered. She couldn't have caught him. She was in the stands. He was a hundred feet up. There was nothing she could possibly have done.

She kept running anyway.

Then there was a flash of silver on the pitch. Professor Dumbledore was striding across the grass with his wand raised, and Harry's fall slowed as if the air had turned to honey, and he drifted the last few feet to the ground and lay still in the mud.

Pansy caught her by the arm at the bottom of the stands.

"Draconia." Pansy was out of breath. Her eyes were enormous. "Draconia, what are you doing?"

Draconia stared down at her own hands. They were trembling so hard she couldn't stop them.

"Nothing," she said. "I wanted a better view."

Pansy didn't say anything. She just kept holding onto Draconia's arm, very tightly, until the teachers had carried Harry off the pitch.

Draconia would never know that Pansy never told anyone about that day. Not a single soul. Not ever.

✦ ✦ ✦

His broom was destroyed. It had blown into the Whomping Willow, and the tree had smashed it to splinters.

Draconia should have been delighted. For a whole week she tried to be.

Then, on Christmas morning, somebody sent Harry Potter a Firebolt.

The Firebolt. The fastest broom in the world, faster even than her Nimbus. It cost more than most wizards earned in a year. It came with no note.

She heard about it at lunch and put down her fork.

"Who sent it?"

"Nobody knows," said Pansy. "No card. McGonagall's taken it away to check it for curses."

"Nobody sends a broom like that without a card." Draconia's voice was rising. She couldn't stop it. "Nobody spends that kind of money on a Gryffindor for no reason. Somebody must be..." She stopped.

Somebody must like him.

Some girl. Some rich, secret, anonymous girl, who could send a Firebolt without even signing it, who'd be more generous and more mysterious and more romantic than a Sugar Quill in a box with a family crest on it...

"Draconia," said Pansy carefully, "you've bent your spoon."

She looked down. She had.

"I could have bought him one," she said, to nobody. "I could have bought him three."

Pansy, very wisely, said nothing at all.

Chapter Six: The Slap

The Firebolt had come back to Harry Potter in February, and a week after the Ravenclaw match, Draconia watched Gryffindor practise with it from the top of the empty stands.

She told Vivienne and Greta she was scouting. For Slytherin. Obviously.

She had a notebook. She had even written things in it. Accelerates very fast, said the first line. Turns very tight, said the second. The third line said his hair looks completely ridiculous in the wind, and she crossed it out so hard that her quill went through the page.

The Firebolt was beautiful. She could see that even from the stands: sleek and dark and polished, with a tail of birch twigs so perfectly trimmed it looked combed. And Harry flew it the way other people breathed. He dropped from the top of the goalposts to an inch above the grass and back again in the time it took her to blink, laughing out loud, the sound carrying all the way up to her through the cold air.

Nobody laughed like that on a broom unless they loved it more than almost anything.

She put her notebook down.

At the end of practice, the Gryffindors trooped off toward the changing rooms, and Harry landed, and looked up. Straight at the top of the stands. Straight at her.

He'd known she was there the whole time.

He raised one hand. It wasn't a wave, exactly. It was more like a salute.

Come and try it, it said. If you think you can.

Draconia sat very still and felt her heart go, hard, like a fist on a door.

"Scouting," she told the empty stands. "I'm scouting."

✦ ✦ ✦

The next Hogsmeade weekend fell in the middle of a cold snap, with snow still thick on the ground, and Draconia took Vivienne and Greta up the lane past the village to look at the Shrieking Shack.

It was the most haunted building in Britain, supposedly. It sat on a rise beyond a broken fence, boarded up and sagging, and people said that screams and howls came from it on certain nights. Draconia thought it looked like a shed with ideas above its station.

Ron Weasley was standing at the fence, alone, with his hands in his pockets.

She knew she should walk past. She'd been trying, lately. Trying not to say things.

But he was there, and he was alone, and Vivienne nudged her, and it was so much easier to be what everyone expected.

"Admiring the view, Weasley?" she called. "I suppose you'd like to live somewhere like that. It's probably bigger than your house."

Ron turned round slowly, ears already going red.

And a huge ball of wet mud and snow came out of absolutely nowhere and hit Draconia full in the back of the head.

She shrieked. She spun round, dripping. The lane behind her was empty. The snow was smooth and untouched. There was nobody there.

A second one hit Greta on the ear. A third hit Vivienne in the stomach, and she sat down in the snow with a thump.

"Who's doing that?" Draconia screamed, clawing slush out of her collar. It was running down her back. It was in her hair, her long golden hair, which she had spent twenty minutes on that morning. "Show yourself, you coward!"

Something invisible stepped on a twig. Something invisible laughed, a very familiar laugh. Then a great clump of snow detached itself from the empty air in front of her and landed on top of her head like a hat.

And then, just for a second, as though someone had tripped on the hem of something, a head appeared, floating in midair about five feet off the ground.

Untidy black hair. Round glasses. Green eyes, bright with glee.

It vanished again at once.

Draconia stood in the snow, dripping mud from her eyelashes, with her mouth open.

Vivienne was scrambling to her feet. "Did you see that? There was a head! A floating head! We have to tell Snape, we have to..."

"There wasn't anything," said Draconia.

Vivienne stopped. "But..."

"I didn't see anything," said Draconia, very clearly. "Did you, Greta?"

Greta looked at her. Greta was not clever, but she was not stupid either, and she had known Draconia for a very long time.

"No," said Greta slowly. "Nothing."

"It was probably Peeves," said Draconia. She wiped a lump of mud from her cheek with enormous dignity, turned round, and marched back down the hill with her friends trailing behind her, leaving Ron Weasley at the fence with his mouth hanging open.

She didn't tell Snape. She didn't tell anyone.

She had worked it out, standing there in the snow. Harry Potter wasn't allowed in Hogsmeade. If Snape found out he'd been there, he would be in more trouble than he'd ever been in his life. Every teacher in the castle was already watching him because of Sirius Black.

One word from her, and he'd be finished.

She just didn't say it.

✦ ✦ ✦

She did, however, stop him on the stairs to the Owlery three days later, when he was alone.

"Nice head, Potter," she said. "Shame about the rest of you."

He went very still. "I don't know what you..."

"You got mud in my hair." She tossed it over her shoulder. It had taken two whole evenings and a borrowed potion of Pansy's to get it gold again instead of brown. "Do you have any idea how long it takes to look like this?"

He stared at her.

"You didn't tell Snape," he said slowly.

"Tell him what? That I was attacked by a snowstorm?" She sniffed. "I have my dignity."

She started down the stairs past him.

"Malfoy." He caught her sleeve. Not hard. Just enough.

She stopped. She looked down at his hand on her sleeve. He took it away quickly.

"Thanks," he said.

"I didn't do anything," said Draconia, and went on down the stairs, and was very glad the stairwell was so dark, because her face had turned the color of a Gryffindor scarf.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Quidditch Cup came down to the last match of the year: Slytherin against Gryffindor.

Everyone had been waiting for it for months. The corridors were so tense that fights broke out between lessons. Slytherins hexed Gryffindors in the library; Gryffindors hexed Slytherins in the Great Hall. Marcus Flint gave a team talk in the changing rooms that was mostly about which Gryffindor players it would be acceptable to break.

"And Malfoy." Flint jabbed a thick finger at her. "Potter's got the Firebolt. You won't outfly him. So when he goes for the Snitch, you stop him. Whatever it takes. Understand?"

Draconia tightened the strap on her glove and said nothing.

The day was brilliant, sunlit and blue, with a light breeze. The whole school was packed into the stands, three-quarters of it in scarlet and a quarter in green, and the noise when the teams walked out was like a physical wall.

Harry walked out onto the pitch with the Firebolt over his shoulder. He glanced along the Slytherin line. His eyes met hers.

He nodded, once. Good luck.

She lifted her chin. I don't need it.

Madam Hooch blew the whistle, and fourteen players shot up into the blue.

It was the dirtiest game Draconia had ever played in. Flint rammed the Gryffindor Chasers every chance he got. The Slytherin Beaters aimed Bludgers at people's heads when the Quaffle was on the other side of the pitch. Penalty after penalty went to Gryffindor, and Gryffindor scored most of them, and the score climbed and climbed, while Draconia stayed high above it all with Harry, circling, watching each other, watching the sky.

He was always a little faster. Wherever she flew, he was there first. Whenever she turned, he had already turned. Once, she dived at nothing at all, pretending she'd seen the Snitch, just to see what he'd do, and he dived with her and pulled up level with her six feet from the ground, and laughed.

"Nice try," he called.

"It was worth a try," she shouted back, and was horrified to discover she was laughing too.

He saw the Snitch before she did.

She saw him go. She saw the Firebolt tip down and streak across the pitch like a thrown spear, low over the grass toward the far goalposts, where a tiny flicker of gold was hovering, and she knew, the way she had known in her bones on the day of the Remembrall, that she was never going to catch him. Not fairly.

She flattened herself along her Nimbus and went after him with everything she had.

She got close. She got closer than she ever had before. She was at his heels, at his broom's tail, the perfect birch twigs a foot from her face, and she heard Flint roaring from somewhere above her, STOP HIM, MALFOY!...

And she reached out, and grabbed the tail of his Firebolt.

She didn't decide to. Her hand simply did it. For one terrible, glorious second she was holding on, and the Firebolt jerked and slowed, and Harry twisted round, furious and astonished, and the whole Gryffindor stand screamed...

She let go.

Nobody made her. She just looked at her own hand, clutching his broom like a thief's, and opened her fingers.

The Firebolt leapt forward like an arrow off a string. Harry flattened himself, stretched out his arm, and closed his hand around the Snitch.

The stadium detonated.

Gryffindor had won the Quidditch Cup for the first time in seven years. The crowd poured onto the pitch in a flood of scarlet and lifted Harry onto their shoulders. The Slytherin team landed around her in a furious knot of green, and Flint was shouting at her with his face gone purple.

"You had him! You HAD him and you LET GO!"

"Madam Hooch was about to give Gryffindor a penalty for it anyway," said Draconia coldly. "We'd have lost regardless."

It wasn't true, and they both knew it.

She walked off the pitch alone. At the edge of the crowd, she looked back once.

Harry was up on the Gryffindors' shoulders with the Snitch in one hand and the Cup in the other, his hair wild, his face shining. And even from there, in the middle of all that noise, he found her. He looked straight at her across the sea of scarlet, and at first he looked puzzled.

Then he looked as though he understood.

She turned away before she had to see what came after that.

✦ ✦ ✦

He found her by the lake that evening.

She'd gone down there to be alone, because the Slytherin common room had turned into a pit of furious whispering, and every conversation stopped dead when she walked in. She was sitting on a flat rock near the water's edge with her knees drawn up and her Quidditch robes still on, watching the giant squid roll one lazy tentacle in the last of the sunlight.

She heard footsteps on the grass. She didn't turn round. She knew who walked like that: fast, a bit uneven, as if he were always about to break into a run.

"Shouldn't you be at your party?" she said. "I can hear it from here. The whole of Gryffindor Tower is singing."

"They won't notice I've gone for ten minutes." Harry stopped a few feet away. He still had grass stains on his knees. "Probably."

A long pause. The squid sank beneath the surface with a soft plop.

"You let go," said Harry.

"My hand slipped."

"It didn't slip. I saw you. You were holding on, and you looked at your hand, and you let go." He sounded honestly bewildered. "Flint's going to murder you. Why?"

Draconia looked at the water for a long time. She twisted one of her gold hoops round and round.

"Because you'd have won anyway," she said at last. "Eventually. You always do. And I didn't want to win like..." She stopped. Like my father, she didn't say. Like the kind of person who grabs. "I didn't want to win like that."

Harry didn't say anything.

"And you caught me once," she added, very fast, before she could lose her nerve. "Last year. In the rain. You let the Snitch go." She lifted one shoulder. "So now we're even. That's all it was. A debt. Malfoys pay their debts."

The silence stretched so long that she finally had to look at him.

He was smiling. Not the crooked, private smile from the Great Hall. Something smaller and softer, which she had never seen before and didn't know what to do with.

"We're not even," he said.

"We are..."

"You didn't tell Snape about Hogsmeade," said Harry. "You gave me the chocolates. And back in second year, at Christmas, you told..." He stopped abruptly. His ears went red.

Draconia frowned at him. "Told who what?"

"Nothing," said Harry, far too quickly. "Nothing. I just mean you keep... doing things. And pretending you didn't." He shoved his hands in his pockets. "So we're not even. I owe you about four."

"Four," said Draconia faintly.

"At least."

She didn't know what to say. She had never once in her life not known what to say, and now it kept happening, and it was always him.

"Go back to your party, Potter," she managed at last. "Before they send out a search party and find you talking to a Slytherin and have you committed."

He grinned, and turned, and jogged away up the dark lawn toward the lights of the castle.

He was halfway up the hill when he turned round and called back, "Nice flying, Malfoy!"

And she sat on her rock by the lake until long after the stars came out, with her face pressed into her knees, smiling so hard it hurt.

✦ ✦ ✦

Her father won. Of course he did.

Buckbeak was tried by the Committee for the Disposal of Dangerous Creatures, and her father sat in the gallery in his silver-headed cane and his finest robes, and the hippogriff was sentenced to death.

Draconia got the letter at breakfast. She read it twice.

Execution to be carried out at sunset on the sixth of June.

She'd thought she would feel triumphant. She felt sick.

She wrote to him that same night. Father, my arm is completely fine. It doesn't even have a scar. Perhaps the Committee should know that. Perhaps it doesn't need to...

His answer came in two days.

Draconia. This was never about your arm.

She read that line for a long time. She thought about her father, smiling his pleasant smile at the Committee. About Hagrid, whom her father despised. About Dumbledore, who had hired him, whom her father despised more.

It was never about your arm.

It was about using it.

She folded the letter up very small and put it in the fire.

✦ ✦ ✦

The week before the appeal, she found Hermione Granger asleep in the library.

It was late. The library was nearly empty. Hermione was slumped over a table at the very back, her cheek on an open book, surrounded by a small fortress of enormous, dusty volumes: Hippogriff Psychology. Fowl or Foul? A Study of Hippogriff Brutality. Precedents in Magical Creature Law, 1650 to the Present. There were pages and pages of notes in her small, neat handwriting, and a quill had rolled out of her hand and left a long ink line across the last one.

She had been building Buckbeak's defense. On her own. For months.

Draconia stood between the shelves and looked at her for a long time.

There was a spare cloak draped over the next chair. Probably Ron Weasley's; it was far too big and had a hole in the elbow. Draconia picked it up. She held it for a moment, hovering, while Hermione breathed slow and deep and exhausted with ink on her fingers.

Then Madam Pince came round the corner with her lamp, and Draconia dropped the cloak back on the chair and walked quickly away before anyone could see.

She thought about that ink line all the way back to the dungeons.

✦ ✦ ✦

On the sixth of June, just before sunset, she went down to Hagrid's hut.

She didn't know what she was going to say. She had some idea of telling Hagrid that she was sorry. That she hadn't meant for it to go this far. That she'd written to her father, that she'd tried.

She brought Vivienne and Greta, because she didn't go anywhere without them, and that was her first mistake.

Because they got to the top of the slope above the hut, and she could hear Hagrid sobbing inside, great heaving sobs you could hear from fifty yards away, and Vivienne snickered.

And Draconia, who had been Draconia Malfoy for thirteen years and had never once said sorry to anyone, felt the old armor snap shut around her before she could stop it.

"Listen to him," she heard herself say, with a curl of her lip. "Blubbering like a baby. Over a chicken."

"You."

Hermione Granger came out from behind the trees on the path. Harry and Ron were right behind her. All three of them had been crying. Hermione's face was blotched and red, and her eyes were blazing, and she was walking straight at Draconia with her fists clenched.

"Don't you dare," Hermione said. "Don't you dare stand there and laugh. He's lost everything. He loved that creature, and your father..."

"My father," said Draconia, lifting her chin, "was only doing his duty..."

The slap was so hard it spun her head sideways.

For a moment, the world went white. Her ear rang. Her cheek burned as if it had been set on fire. One of her gold hoops had been knocked clean out of her ear, and she heard it bounce, tink, on a stone somewhere in the grass.

She put her hand to her face. It came away without blood, which surprised her. It felt like there should be blood.

Nobody had ever hit her in her life.

Nobody had ever dared.

Vivienne and Greta were gaping. Ron Weasley's mouth was hanging open. And Harry...

She couldn't look at Harry.

"Come on," she said to Vivienne and Greta. Her voice came out high and strange. "Come on. Let's go."

She turned. Then she turned back, because she had to say something, she had to, she was a Malfoy and Malfoys always had the last word.

"You wait," she said to Hermione. Her voice was shaking. "You wait. I'll have you for this."

Then she walked away up the slope as fast as she could without running, and she did not cry. Not in front of them. She would rather have died.

✦ ✦ ✦

She cried in the Owlery.

It was the only place she could think of where nobody would come. It was full of sleepy owls and straw and draughts, and she sat on the cold stone floor under a window with her knees pulled up, and held her burning cheek, and cried until she couldn't breathe.

Not because it hurt, though it did.

Because she'd deserved it.

She had walked down that hill to say sorry. And instead she had stood there and called a heartbroken man a baby, and she had defended her father, who had done something terrible and pretended it was duty, and she'd done it because Vivienne snickered. Because it was easier than being brave.

Granger had seen straight through her. So had Harry. So, probably, had the owls.

I owe you about four, he'd said by the lake, only three weeks ago. She wondered how many he thought he owed her now. Zero, probably. Less than zero. There wasn't a number low enough for what she'd said on that hill, in front of him, about a man he loved.

I'll have you, she'd said. I'll have you for this.

She said it again now, to herself, very quietly, and it sounded completely different up here, in the dark, with nobody listening.

It sounded like a promise that she would be better than this.

Somewhere below, sunset came, and went. She heard later that the execution never happened. Buckbeak had vanished from Hagrid's garden into thin air, just before the axe could fall, and nobody could work out how.

Draconia never found out either.

But when she heard, she went back to the Owlery on her own and sat in the same spot under the window, and laughed until she cried again, for a completely different reason.

✦ ✦ ✦

Her missing earring came back the next day.

It arrived at breakfast, brought by a scruffy school owl and wrapped in a small folded square of parchment. The handwriting was untidy, cramped, and slanted, and it said only:

Found this on the path. Thought you'd want it back.

There was no name.

She knew exactly whose it was anyway. She'd been staring at that handwriting over the tops of Potions books for three years.

She put the hoop back in her ear. She put the parchment inside her Transfiguration book, pressed flat between two pages where nobody would ever look.

And she didn't say anything about it to anyone. She didn't even say anything about it to herself.

But she touched that earring about a hundred times on the train home.

Chapter Seven: The Ferret

The Top Box at the Quidditch World Cup was the best seat in the stadium, and Draconia Malfoy had been looking forward to it all summer.

It was high above everything, right at the center line, level with the goalposts, with soft purple-and-gold chairs and a view of a hundred thousand witches and wizards filling the tiers below like a sea. Her father had paid for a great many things at the Ministry that year. The Top Box was one of the things he'd got back.

She had new dress robes for it. Pale green silk, the exact green of her eyes, with a silver clasp at the throat. Her mother had done her hair herself, a long golden braid pinned in a crown. Her gold hoops had been polished until they shone.

She walked into the box behind her parents with her chin high, fully expecting to be the most elegant person in it.

And stopped.

Because the front row of the Top Box was entirely full of Weasleys.

There were so many of them. Red heads everywhere, in a row, like a line of lit matches. And in the middle of the row, between Ron Weasley and Hermione Granger, wearing a jumper that was clearly one of Mrs. Weasley's and his hair doing something truly catastrophic in the wind, sat Harry Potter.

He turned round. He saw her.

His eyes went, very quickly, from her face, to the crown of braids, to the green silk, and back to her face again.

He didn't say anything. He just blinked. Twice.

Good, thought Draconia, with deep satisfaction, and sailed past him with her nose in the air.

"Arthur," said her father, in the voice that was pleasant and cold at the same time. "The Top Box. How generous of the Ministry. I had no idea they were giving seats away with the breakfast cereal now."

Mr. Weasley went red. Her father smiled his thin smile, let his eyes rest for a pointed moment on Hermione Granger, and then turned away to shake hands with the Minister for Magic.

Draconia sat down beside her mother, two seats along and one row back from Harry. She did not look at the back of his head at all.

She could see his ears had gone red, though. She noticed that entirely by accident.

✦ ✦ ✦

The match began with the mascots.

Bulgaria's mascots were Veela.

Draconia had heard of them, but she'd never seen one. A hundred women glided out onto the pitch, and they were the most beautiful creatures she had ever seen: their skin glowed like the moon, their silver-white hair floated around them without any wind, and when the music started, they began to dance.

It was very pretty, she supposed. She was trying to decide whether their hair was actually nicer than hers, and concluding that it was, a little, and being quietly annoyed about it, when she noticed that Harry Potter was climbing onto the front of the box.

Not leaning over. Climbing. He had one leg over the wall. He had a dazed, blissful, completely empty expression on his face, and he was staring down at the dancing Veela like a man who'd seen the sun for the first time, and he looked extremely likely to jump.

Beside him, Ron was doing the same thing.

Draconia stood up, reached forward over the seat in front of her, grabbed a fistful of the back of Harry's borrowed jumper, and yanked.

He toppled backward into his chair with a thud. The music stopped. The Veela left the pitch. Harry blinked and looked round, bewildered, like someone waking up from a nap.

He found her standing right behind him, still holding a handful of his jumper.

"What," said Harry, "just happened?"

"You were about to jump off the Top Box," Draconia informed him icily. "For a dancer."

His ears went from red to purple. "I wasn't..."

"You were drooling, Potter."

"I was not..."

"Draconia," said her mother mildly, from behind her. "Do sit down, darling. You're holding the Potter boy's clothing."

Draconia let go of the jumper as if it had burned her, and sat down, and spent the next ten minutes looking at the sky with enormous concentration while Ron Weasley snorted into his programme.

✦ ✦ ✦

But she forgot all about it once the match began.

She had never seen flying like it. Not even Harry's. The Irish Chasers moved like one creature with three heads. The Bulgarian Seeker, Viktor Krum, was hunched and sullen on the ground, but in the air he was something else altogether: fast and savage and absolutely fearless.

Halfway through, Krum went into a dive.

It was a straight, suicidal plunge, a hundred feet down, and the Irish Seeker dived after him, and the whole stadium screamed, and Draconia was on her feet with her hands over her mouth before she knew it. Then, at the very last second, Krum pulled up. The Irish Seeker didn't. He hit the ground with a thud that shook the stands.

There had never been a Snitch there at all. Krum had faked it.

"Wronski Feint," said Draconia and Harry at exactly the same moment, in exactly the same breathless voice.

They looked at each other.

Harry was on his feet too, gripping the front of the box. His face was lit up in that way it only ever was in the air. For one second, the Top Box, and her father, and the Weasleys, and every awful thing she'd ever said to him, simply fell away, and there were only two Seekers who both knew exactly what they'd just watched, and how impossible it was, and how badly they wanted to try it.

Then her father said "Draconia," very quietly, and she sat down.

She didn't look at Harry again for the rest of the match.

But she felt him, the whole time, two seats along and one row down, like a warm spot in the sun.

✦ ✦ ✦

That night, the campsite burned.

She was asleep in her family's tent, the one with the silk walls and the proper beds, when the shouting started. By the time she got outside, there were people running everywhere between the tents, screaming, and on the far side of the field a crowd was marching.

They were wizards in dark hooded cloaks, with masks over their faces, walking in a tight, laughing mass with their wands pointed up into the sky. And floating above them, high in the air, turning helplessly, were four figures. A Muggle man and his wife. And two small children, upside down, their nightclothes falling over their faces.

Draconia stood outside her tent in her dressing gown and watched them.

She knew how her father walked.

She'd known it her whole life. The particular length of his stride. The way he held his shoulders. The way his left hand turned, just slightly, when he raised his wand.

"Draconia." Her mother appeared out of the smoke beside her. Narcissa's face was white and very calm. "Go into the woods. Wait there. Don't come back until I come for you."

"Mother, is Father..."

"Go."

She went.

✦ ✦ ✦

It was dark among the trees, and cold, and full of other frightened people stumbling about, and she hadn't walked very far before she almost walked into three figures standing together in the shadow of an oak.

She knew them before her eyes had adjusted. She would have known that hair anywhere.

"Malfoy." Ron Weasley's voice was hard. "What are you doing here?"

"The same as you, Weasley. Standing in a forest in my nightclothes." She wrapped her arms around herself. Then she looked past Ron. At Hermione. Hermione was standing a little behind Harry, her bushy hair full of twigs, her face pale.

And Draconia, before she could decide not to, said, "You need to get her out of here."

They all stared at her.

"Granger." Draconia's voice came out too sharp, too fast. She couldn't slow it down. "They're looking for Muggle-borns. That's what the masks are for. That's who they're hunting. If they see her, they'll put her up there with those Muggles and spin her upside down for fun. So get her away from here. Now. Deeper into the trees. Stop standing about in the open like idiots."

The silence was very long.

"Is your dad up there?" said Ron quietly. "In a mask?"

Draconia didn't answer.

She looked at Harry instead. She didn't mean to. He was watching her in the dark, very steadily. His eyes went to her face, and stayed there, and she knew that he could see it. All of it. That she didn't know. That she did know. That she was frightened of the answer.

"Come on," Harry said to the others, not taking his eyes off her. "She's right. Let's go."

"She's right?" said Ron.

"Ron. Come on."

They went. Hermione looked back once over her shoulder, a strange, searching look, as though she was trying to work out a very difficult sum.

Draconia stayed under the oak with her arms wrapped around herself, and she waited, and a few minutes later, a voice somewhere in the woods shouted a word she didn't know, and a vast green light burst open in the sky above the trees.

It was a skull. An enormous, glittering green skull, made of stars, with a serpent sliding out of its mouth like a tongue.

Draconia had seen it once before.

She had been six. She'd walked into her father's dressing room without knocking, and he'd been rolling down his left sleeve, and just for a moment she'd seen something inked on the inside of his forearm, faded and grey. He'd pulled his sleeve down very quickly. He'd never said anything about it. She'd never asked.

She stood under the oak and looked up at the skull in the sky, and all around her in the forest, people began to scream.

✦ ✦ ✦

Nobody at Hogwarts talked about anything else for the first week of term.

The Dark Mark. The masked marchers. The Ministry saying nothing. Draconia's father had written to her once, a smooth, pleasant letter about how regrettable the whole business had been, and she had read it three times looking for something that wasn't there, and burned it.

And then, on the Thursday, on the steps of the entrance hall, Ron Weasley said it out loud.

"Wonder why your dad wasn't in the paper, Malfoy," he said, loudly enough for the whole crowd queueing for lunch to hear. "Everyone else's dad was. Must've been hard to recognize him. You know. What with the mask."

Somebody laughed.

Draconia's face went hot. Then cold. Then something else, something that wasn't a temperature at all.

Ron turned his back on her and started up the stairs.

And she pulled out her wand and pointed it straight at the back of his head.

She never knew, afterward, what she'd been about to cast. She didn't even know then. Her mind was just a white roar. But before the spell had left her lips, someone stepped sideways across the line between her wand and Ron's back.

Harry.

He'd seen her draw. He hadn't drawn his own. He just stood there on the step, in the way, looking down at her.

"Don't," he said quietly.

It was that single word, in that quiet voice, as if she was someone worth saying it to, that made her hand shake. The spell went wide. It exploded against the stone wall with a crack and a burst of white sparks, three feet from Harry's shoulder...

"NOT ON MY WATCH, GIRL!"

There was a second bang, a blinding flash, and Draconia's whole world turned upside down.

Everything was suddenly very large. The flagstones were enormous. The crowd towered above her. She tried to shout, and all that came out was a high, furious little squeak, and she looked down at herself and saw small pale-gold paws, and a long, sleek, pale-gold body, and a tail.

She was a ferret.

"A wand at a boy's back!" Professor Moody came stumping down the marble staircase, his magical blue eye whirling, his wooden leg clunking on every step, his wand pointed straight at her. "Of all the gutless tricks! Let's see how you like being thrown about!"

He flicked his wand. Draconia shot ten feet into the air, and fell, and hit the floor, and bounced straight back up again.

The crowd howled.

Up, and down. Up, and down. The ceiling. The floor. The ceiling. Her legs flailing in the air, her tail whirling like a windmill, the whole of the entrance hall screaming with laughter, and she could hear the Slytherins laughing too, Pansy was laughing, even Greta...

"Professor, stop it!"

Someone was shouting over the noise. Someone was standing right in front of Moody.

"Stop it! Put her down! She didn't hit anyone!"

It was Harry.

Moody's magical eye swivelled round to stare at him. "She was aiming at your friend's back, Potter."

"I know. And I stopped her, and she missed. Put her down."

Draconia hung in midair, spinning slowly, upside down, a small furious pale-gold ferret, and stared at Harry Potter through the curtain of her own tail.

"PROFESSOR MOODY!"

Professor McGonagall was coming down the stairs at a run, her arms full of books. "What... what are you doing?"

"Correcting," said Moody.

"Correct... Alastor, is that a child?"

"Mostly," said Moody.

McGonagall shrieked, and drew her wand, and a moment later there was a loud crack, and Draconia was lying on the flagstones in a heap, human again.

Her hair was everywhere. The ribbon was gone. Her robes were twisted around her legs. Her face was burning pink. One of her gold hoops was hanging half out of her ear.

She sat up slowly, in the middle of a circle of three hundred staring students, with McGonagall scolding Moody over her head, and she felt her eyes sting and her throat close and her whole body burn with the worst humiliation of her entire life.

And then a hand came down in front of her face.

It was holding her missing hair ribbon.

She looked up. Harry was crouched beside her. He didn't say anything. He didn't smile, and he didn't laugh. He just held out the ribbon.

She took it.

He stood up, and turned, and walked up the marble stairs after Ron, and didn't look back.

Draconia sat on the floor of the entrance hall with a green silk ribbon crumpled in her fist, and all the laughter in the world couldn't have made her feel as small as that one quiet kindness did.

✦ ✦ ✦

On Halloween night, the Goblet of Fire chose the Hogwarts champion for the Triwizard Tournament: Cedric Diggory of Hufflepuff.

And then, a moment later, when everyone was already cheering, it spat out a fourth name.

Harry Potter.

"He cheated," Pansy hissed, in the common room afterwards. The whole of Slytherin was in uproar. "He's fourteen. There's an age line. He must have got some older student to put his name in, the little show-off, he just can't stand not being the center of..."

"He didn't," said Draconia.

Pansy turned. "What?"

"He didn't put his name in." Draconia was sitting in her chair by the fire, and she was surprised at how calm she sounded. "Did you see his face when they called it out? He went white as paper. He looked like someone had hit him." She shrugged. "He can't lie, Pansy. I told you before. He goes red at the ears. He didn't go red. He went white."

"Well," said Pansy, after a pause, "you'd know."

The next morning, the whole of Slytherin House was wearing badges.

They were large and round and glowed a bright, lurid red, and they said SUPPORT CEDRIC DIGGORY, THE REAL HOGWARTS CHAMPION. And when you pressed them, the words vanished and new ones blazed out in green:

POTTER STINKS.

Pansy pressed one into Draconia's hand at breakfast. "Here. We made them last night. Everyone's got one."

Draconia looked at it.

Everyone had got one. The entire Slytherin table was glowing red and green. She could feel them all watching to see what she'd do.

She pinned it on.

Harry came into the Great Hall a minute later. He was pale and tired, and people were whispering as he passed, and he was walking with his head down, and then someone at the Slytherin table pressed their badge and it flashed POTTER STINKS right at him, and then another, and another, a whole table of green flashing words...

And his eyes found her. Went to her badge.

He didn't frown. His face just went a tiny bit more closed, the way a door closes. As if he'd expected nothing else.

Draconia looked down at her toast. She felt sick.

✦ ✦ ✦

That night, in the dormitory, after Pansy had fallen asleep, she sat up in bed with her badge in her lap and her wand in her hand.

It took her an hour and a half and most of a library book on Charms. It was, frankly, some of the finest spellwork she'd ever done.

At breakfast the next morning, she was the only person at the Slytherin table whose badge said something different.

It still glowed red. It still said SUPPORT CEDRIC DIGGORY. When you pressed it, the green words still flashed.

But hers said:

POTTER'S HAIR STINKS.

Pansy stared at it for a very long moment. "That's not..."

"It's accurate," said Draconia.

Harry came into the Hall. Somebody pressed a badge at him. He braced, and turned his head...

And saw hers.

He stopped dead in the middle of the Great Hall. She watched him read it. She watched his mouth twitch. She watched him try very hard not to laugh, and fail, and turn it into a cough, and put a hand over his mouth, and walk on to the Gryffindor table with his shoulders shaking.

Draconia ate her porridge with great dignity and did not smile at all.

It was the best breakfast she'd had in weeks.

✦ ✦ ✦

It lasted until lunch.

She was halfway through a plate of shepherd's pie when she realized that Harry wasn't eating. He was sitting very still at the Gryffindor table, not listening to Ron, not listening to anyone, just looking at something across the Hall with a slightly dazed, slightly pink expression, a little like the one he'd had on the front of the Top Box.

She followed his gaze.

At the Ravenclaw table sat a girl. She was a year older than them, with long, shining dark hair and a lovely, gentle face. She was laughing at something one of her friends had said, and when she laughed, she tucked her hair behind her ear.

Draconia knew who she was. Everyone did. She was the Ravenclaw Seeker, and she was very good.

Her name was Cho Chang.

Draconia put her fork down.

She looked at Cho's shining dark hair. Then, before she could stop herself, she picked up her spoon and looked at her own face in the back of it: long, pale, all chin, upside down and distorted, with straight golden hair and a scowl.

Across the Hall, Cho laughed again. Harry went a shade pinker.

"Draconia?" said Pansy. "Are you all right? You look like you want to hex someone."

"I'm perfectly fine," said Draconia.

She was not fine.

She had never met Cho Chang in her life. Cho Chang had never done a single thing to her. By every reasonable standard, Cho Chang was a perfectly nice person.

Draconia decided, on the spot, that she hated her.

Chapter Eight: Ask Properly

The letter from her father came on a Tuesday, and it wasn't about her at all.

It was a long, smooth, grumbling letter about the Ministry. About Ludo Bagman, who was running the Triwizard Tournament, and who her father considered an idiot. About money being wasted. About recklessness. And halfway down the second page, in the middle of a paragraph about the budget, one line:

They have imported four fully grown dragons from Romania for the first task, if you can believe it. Nesting mothers. Barely controlled. Somebody is going to be killed, and I shall enjoy watching Bagman explain it.

Draconia read the line four times.

Then she folded the letter very carefully and sat quite still in her chair by the common room fire, while the lake moved green and black against the windows, and thought: Dragons.

Four champions. Four dragons.

One of the champions was fourteen years old and had never faced anything bigger than a hippogriff.

✦ ✦ ✦

She told herself she wasn't going to tell him.

It wasn't her business. He was a Gryffindor. He'd probably find out anyway. Every other champion certainly would; Durmstrang and Beauxbatons would cheat, everyone knew that, and Cedric Diggory was a Hufflepuff and so frightfully decent that someone would tell him out of pure pity.

Nobody would tell Harry.

Half the school thought he'd cheated his way into the tournament. The other half were wearing badges. Even Ron Weasley wasn't speaking to him; they'd had some enormous row, and for two weeks Harry had been walking around the castle alone, eating alone, sitting alone in the back of lessons with his jaw set and his shoulders up around his ears.

She'd watched him do it. She'd watched him every single day.

On Thursday, she found him in the library.

He was at a table in the far corner, surrounded by books, with his head in his hands. She walked up to the table and stopped, and he looked up, and his face went instantly, wearily guarded, the way it did with everyone now.

"Come to tell me I stink?" he said.

"Your hair stinks." She sat down opposite him without being invited. She lowered her voice. "Potter. The first task. It's dragons."

He stared at her.

"I know," he said slowly. "Hagrid showed me. On Saturday night." His eyes narrowed. "How do you know?"

"My father mentioned it." She lifted her chin. "In passing."

Harry looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him understand what she was doing: sitting in the library, in front of everyone, telling the boy the whole of Slytherin was wearing badges against exactly what he was going to face.

"Why are you telling me?" he said quietly.

"Because I'd hate for you to be eaten before I've had the chance to beat you properly at Quidditch." She glanced at his pile of books. Dragon Species of Great Britain and Ireland. From Egg to Inferno. "Those are useless."

"Thanks."

"No. Listen." She leaned forward. "What are you reading about? Stunning them? Blinding them? You're not going to out-curse a dragon, Potter. You're fourteen. You'd need six wizards and a very long afternoon." She tapped the table. "What are you good at? The one thing? The thing nobody in this entire school can do as well as you?"

He frowned. "I don't..."

"You're a Seeker," said Draconia impatiently. "Stop thinking like a Gryffindor and think like a Seeker. You don't fight a dragon. You outfly it."

Harry opened his mouth. Closed it.

"They won't let me take a broom in there," he said. "It's only wands."

"Then use your wand," said Draconia, and stood up, "to fetch your broom."

She walked away between the shelves without looking back.

She'd reached the end of the row before she heard him say, very softly, behind her: "Accio."

She smiled all the way down to the dungeons.

✦ ✦ ✦

On the day of the first task, the whole school packed into stands built around a rocky enclosure at the edge of the forest, and one by one, the champions went out to face their dragons.

Cedric Diggory faced a Swedish Short-Snout and got his face burned. Fleur Delacour faced a Welsh Green and got her skirt set on fire. Viktor Krum faced a Chinese Fireball and hit it in the eye with a curse, and it trampled half its own eggs.

Draconia watched all three with her arms folded, pretending to be bored.

Then the fourth dragon came out.

It was black, and scaly, and enormous, and it had bronze spikes all down the length of its tail and yellow eyes like a cat's, and when it opened its mouth, the fire that came out of it scorched the very air. The crowd went silent. Somewhere behind her, a first-year started to cry.

"Hungarian Horntail," said Vivienne, reading the programme. "Says here it's the most dangerous of the lot."

Draconia's arms came unfolded.

The whistle blew. Harry walked out from the champions' tent.

He looked so small. Just a small, thin boy in red robes, standing at the edge of a rocky arena, with his wand in his hand, facing that.

He raised his wand.

She couldn't hear the spell. But she heard, a few seconds later, the whistle of something coming very fast from the direction of the castle. A streak of dark wood and gold. A broom.

The Firebolt shot down into the arena, and Harry swung his leg over it, and kicked off.

And Draconia stopped breathing.

He was magnificent. He flew the way she'd told him to: like a Seeker, not a fighter. He went up, and up, and the Horntail's head went up after him, and he dived and swerved and teased it, drawing it away from its eggs, making it rear and snap and spit fire into the empty sky. It lashed its tail at him. He dodged. It lashed again, and the spikes caught his shoulder, and Draconia heard somebody scream, and realized it was her...

"Draconia!" hissed Pansy, grabbing her arm. "Sit down!"

She was on her feet. She had no idea when she'd stood up. Her nails were dug so hard into her own palms that they'd left red crescents.

Harry was still flying. There was blood on his shoulder, but he was still flying. He drew the Horntail up, up, up, until it spread its wings and lifted its whole enormous body off the eggs to go after him...

And he dropped like a stone, straight down, past its head, and plucked a golden egg off the nest with one hand, and shot away across the arena before the dragon had even realized he'd gone.

The stadium exploded.

Draconia sat down very suddenly. Her legs had stopped working. She found that she was holding onto Greta's sleeve with both hands, and that Greta was looking at her with a sort of dull, patient kindness.

"He's all right," Greta said.

"I know he's all right," Draconia snapped, and let go. "Why wouldn't he be all right? I don't care if he's all right."

Greta said nothing.

Down in the arena, Harry landed. He was clutching the golden egg under his arm. He was grinning like a madman. He looked up at the stands, at the whole roaring crowd, and then his eyes moved along the tiers, past the Gryffindors, past the Ravenclaws...

To the Slytherin stand.

To her.

He lifted the golden egg, just a little. Just for her. Like a salute.

Then Ron Weasley came tearing down out of the stands, pale and shaking, and flung his arms around him, and they were friends again, and the moment was gone.

Draconia looked down at the red crescents in her palms.

"Well," said Pansy, beside her, very quietly. "That was interesting."

✦ ✦ ✦

The Yule Ball was announced a week later.

Snape told the Slytherins himself, in the common room, in the tone of a man reading out a list of the recently deceased. There would be a ball on Christmas night, to celebrate the Tournament. Fourth years and above. Dress robes. Partners.

Partners.

The whole of Slytherin House lost its mind.

For the next fortnight, Hogwarts turned into a hunting ground. People were asked in corridors, in the library, in the middle of meals. Girls went round in giggling packs. Boys went round looking as if they were about to be sick.

Draconia was asked four times in the first week.

Blaise Zabini asked her in the common room with a lazy smile, as if he were doing her a favour. Theodore Nott asked her by letter, which was cowardly. A tall Durmstrang boy with very large eyebrows asked her in the entrance hall in a mixture of English and Bulgarian, and she understood about a third of it. And a Ravenclaw sixth-year she had never spoken to in her life went bright red and stammered his way through the question outside Charms.

She said no to all of them.

"Why?" Pansy demanded, on the fourth. "Blaise is the best-looking boy in our year! What's wrong with him?"

"His robes are last season," said Draconia.

"And Nott?"

"He wrote to me. From twelve feet away."

"And that Ravenclaw? He was sweet!"

"He called me Draconica."

Pansy folded her arms. "Who," she said slowly, "are you waiting for?"

Draconia turned a page of her book that she hadn't been reading.

"Nobody," she said.

Pansy didn't say anything. She didn't have to. She just looked, very deliberately, across the common room toward the stone wall that faced, many floors up, in the direction of Gryffindor Tower.

✦ ✦ ✦

On Thursday, the news reached the dungeons.

Harry Potter had asked Cho Chang to the Yule Ball.

Draconia heard it at breakfast. Millicent said it, carelessly, through a mouthful of toast. Did you hear? Potter asked Cho Chang. Right in front of the Great Hall doors. Went red as a tomato.

Draconia set down her teacup very gently, because otherwise she was going to throw it.

He'd asked Cho. He'd walked up to Cho Chang, who had shining dark hair and a gentle laugh and had never once in her life called him Four Eyes or put him in a pink tutu or said that word in front of him, and he had asked her to the ball.

Of course he had.

What had she expected? What had she possibly...

"And she said no," Millicent went on. "She's going with Cedric Diggory. She already said yes to him."

There was a pause.

Draconia stared at Millicent.

"She said no?"

"Mm."

"To Harry Potter?"

"Mm-hm."

"The Triwizard champion? Who just stole an egg off a Hungarian Horntail?" Draconia's voice was rising. People were turning to look. "Who does she think she is? The nerve! Who in their right mind would say no to..."

She stopped.

The whole Slytherin table was staring at her.

"...to the opportunity," she finished, rather weakly, "of making a Gryffindor look like a fool. In public. She's wasted a golden chance. Honestly. Ravenclaws."

She picked up her teacup again. Her hand was shaking so hard the tea slopped over the side.

He'd asked Cho first.

That was the thing she couldn't get past, walking to Transfiguration with her books clutched against her chest. It didn't matter that Cho said no. He'd asked her. He'd stood in front of the Great Hall doors, gone red as a tomato, and asked a girl to the ball, and that girl had been dark-haired and gentle and pretty and nice.

And not her.

✦ ✦ ✦

That night, she sat on the edge of her bed in front of the tall mirror by the dormitory door, and she tried her hair up.

She tried it in a knot. She tried it in a braid. She tried it in two braids, pinned in loops, the way she'd seen a Beauxbatons girl wear it. She tried it curled, with a charm from Pansy's beauty book that went slightly wrong and turned one side into a frizz like a dandelion clock, and she had to wash it out and start again.

Nothing looked right. Everything looked wrong.

She looked at herself in the mirror with her golden hair loose around her shoulders again and her face pink from scrubbing, and she thought about Cho Chang's dark hair, tucked behind her ear when she laughed.

Her own eyes looked back at her from the glass. Bright green. The same green as...

She looked away.

"Draconia?" Pansy sat up sleepily in the next bed. "What are you doing? It's nearly midnight."

"Nothing."

"You've been in front of that mirror for two hours."

"I'm practising," said Draconia. "For the ball."

"But you haven't got a partner."

Draconia put down her hairbrush.

"I'm aware," she said.

Pansy was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in a strange, gentle voice that Draconia had never heard her use before: "You look nice with it down, you know. You always do. You don't need to do anything to it."

Draconia didn't answer. But she went to bed without touching her hair again.

✦ ✦ ✦

Her dress robes arrived by owl on Saturday morning.

Her mother had chosen them. They came in a long, flat silver box, wrapped in tissue paper that smelled faintly of her mother's perfume, with a card on top in Narcissa's elegant hand: For my girl, who will be the most beautiful one there. Wear the hoops.

Draconia lifted them out, and the Slytherin girls' dormitory went quiet.

They were silk the color of pale gold, the color of her hair in candlelight, with long flowing sleeves and a neckline edged in tiny emeralds. When she held them up, they seemed to catch the green light from the lake and turn it into something warm.

"Oh," said Millicent. "Oh."

"You'll look like a princess," breathed Pansy.

Draconia folded them carefully back into their tissue paper and put the lid back on the box.

It didn't matter. It didn't matter how beautiful they were.

There was nobody to wear them for.

✦ ✦ ✦

The note came on Monday.

It was tucked into her Transfiguration book, between two pages, in exactly the place where she kept the other note, the one from last June, the one that said Found this on the path. She knew then that whoever had put it there knew exactly where she kept things, and there was only one person that could possibly be.

The handwriting was untidy, cramped, and slanted.

Trophy room. Eight o'clock tonight. You can bring a second if you're scared.

She read it standing up in the middle of the Transfiguration classroom while Professor McGonagall was talking, and then she sat down very suddenly and didn't hear another word of the lesson.

The trophy room.

That was where she'd challenged him to a midnight duel, in their very first week, and then told Filch, and never shown up.

You can bring a second if you're scared.

She was scared. She was very, very scared.

She didn't bring a second.

✦ ✦ ✦

The trophy room was dim and cold, lit by two torches, full of glass cases that glittered with cups and shields and plaques and medals. It smelled of metal polish.

He was already there.

He was standing by the case with the Quidditch cups in it, hands shoved in his pockets, looking at the floor. He was wearing his ordinary school robes. His hair was, if anything, worse than usual; it looked like he'd been running his hands through it for an hour.

He looked up when she came in. He looked very relieved, and then very nervous, and then like he was trying to look neither of those things.

"You came," he said.

"I always show up to a duel." She lifted her chin. "Eventually."

"Right. Yeah." He took his hands out of his pockets. Put them back in. Took them out again. "It's not a duel."

"I gathered."

There was a very long silence. Somewhere in one of the glass cases, a small silver cup chimed faintly as if in sympathy.

"So," said Harry. "The ball."

Draconia's heart was going so hard she could feel it in her fingertips.

"The ball," she agreed.

"Are you... do you... I mean." He cleared his throat. His ears had gone scarlet. "Has anyone... You've probably already got someone. You probably have loads of..."

"Four," said Draconia.

He blinked. "Four?"

"People have asked me. Four." She folded her hands in front of her. "I said no to all of them."

"Oh," said Harry. Something in his face changed. "Right. So... do you want to go. With me. To the ball. If you... Would you want to?"

And Draconia Malfoy, who had waited three and a half years for this moment, who had vowed on a train at eleven years old that one day Harry Potter would come to her and ask for something, heard herself say:

"No."

Harry's face fell so fast it was almost funny.

"Right," he said. "Okay. That's... fine. Sorry. I'll just..."

"Not like that." She was trembling now, but she held her chin up. "Ask properly, Potter."

He stared at her.

"Properly," she repeated. "I am not being asked to the Yule Ball by someone mumbling at a glass case with his hands in his pockets. Do it properly or don't do it at all."

For a moment, he looked completely lost.

Then something shifted in his face. He looked at her standing there, very straight, very pale, with her chin up and her gold hoops glinting in the torchlight and her hands clasped so tightly her knuckles had gone white. And she saw him understand. Not just what she was asking for. Why.

He took his hands out of his pockets.

He walked across the trophy room until he was standing right in front of her. And then Harry Potter, the Boy Who Lived, the Triwizard champion, the boy who had refused her hand on the Hogwarts Express, put one hand behind his back and bowed.

It was a terrible bow. He clearly had no idea how to do it. He bent at completely the wrong angle and nearly lost his glasses.

It was the most wonderful thing she had ever seen.

"Miss Malfoy," said Harry, a little breathlessly, straightening up. "Would you do me the honor of going to the Yule Ball with me?"

And then he held out his hand.

Not sideways, the way boys did for shaking.

He held it the way she'd held hers on the train, three and a half years ago. Palm up this time. Waiting. Offering.

Draconia looked at his hand for a long moment. Her eyes stung. She didn't let them.

She put her hand in his.

"Yes," she said, very, very quietly. Barely louder than a breath.

Harry's face broke into a grin so enormous and so relieved it transformed his whole face. His fingers closed around hers, warm and slightly shaking.

They stood there like that for a moment, in the torchlight, surrounded by trophies, holding hands, neither of them quite knowing what to do next.

"You asked Cho first," she said, before she could stop herself.

His grin faltered. His ears went red again.

"Yeah," he said. "I did."

She waited. It hurt. She waited anyway.

"I asked her first," said Harry slowly, "because I wasn't scared of what she'd say." He looked at their hands. "You were the one I was scared to ask."

Draconia couldn't speak.

"I thought you'd laugh," said Harry. "Or hex me. Or say no. And then I'd have to know." He shrugged, awkwardly. "So I asked someone it wouldn't matter with. And when she said no, I was relieved. And that's when I knew I had to ask you. Even if you laughed."

"I didn't laugh," said Draconia, faintly.

"No," said Harry. "You made me bow."

"You bowed terribly."

"I know." He grinned again. "You'll have to teach me. Before Christmas. There's a dance, apparently. The champions have to open it."

"Open it?"

"In front of everyone."

Draconia thought about the whole school watching Harry Potter, who bowed like a collapsing deckchair, attempting to dance.

She thought about the pale gold silk in its silver box.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Eight o'clock. Here. Don't be late, and bring shoes you're prepared to have stepped on."

"Mine stepped on?"

"Obviously yours. I don't make mistakes."

He laughed, and let go of her hand, and she turned and walked out of the trophy room very calmly, very straight-backed, with her chin high and her footsteps perfectly even, all the way along the corridor, around the corner, down three flights of stairs, into the dungeons, past the common room, up the stairs to her dormitory, and into her bed, where she pulled the curtains shut, put her face into her pillow, and screamed.

"Draconia?" Pansy's voice came through the curtains, alarmed. "Draconia, what's wrong?"

Draconia lifted her face from the pillow. It was burning.

"I have a partner," she said.

There was a long silence on the other side of the curtains.

"Is it..."

"Yes."

Pansy shrieked.

Somewhere down the corridor, a door banged open, and someone shouted at them to be quiet, and Draconia lay back on her pillow in the dark with her hand still warm and her heart still hammering, and laughed until she cried.

Chapter Nine: The Yule Ball

Harry Potter could not dance.

This was not a small problem. This was not a case of a few missed steps that could be smoothed over with practice and patience. Harry Potter danced the way a broom handles when you've forgotten to tell it which way is up: enthusiastically, in every direction at once, and with no regard for anybody's feet.

"Ow," said Draconia, for the eleventh time in half an hour.

"Sorry. Sorry! Was that you or me?"

"There are only two of us, Potter. Guess."

It was the first night of lessons, in the trophy room, at eight o'clock. They'd pushed the heavier cases against the walls, and Draconia had borrowed a small enchanted music box from Pansy that played the same three waltzes over and over in a tinny, cheerful voice. The torches flickered. The silver cups watched them from behind their glass like a disapproving audience.

"Again," said Draconia. "From the beginning. Hand here."

She took his right hand and put it on her back, just between her shoulder blades, and she felt him go stiff as a board, as though she'd put his hand on a hot stove.

"It won't bite," she said.

"I know that."

"Then stop holding me like I'm a Blast-Ended Skrewt."

"I'm not..."

"Other hand. Up. Here." She laced her fingers through his left hand and lifted it to shoulder height. His palm was warm and slightly damp. She decided not to mention it, because hers was too. "Now. It goes in threes. One, two, three. One, two, three. You step forward on one. With your left foot. Your other left, Potter..."

He stepped on her toe again.

"Ow."

"Sorry!"

"It's fine," said Draconia through her teeth. "We have eight days."

✦ ✦ ✦

They had eight days, and they used every one of them.

Every evening at eight o'clock, Draconia slipped out of the Slytherin common room with a murmured excuse about the library, and every evening at eight o'clock, Harry was already waiting in the trophy room, and they danced until the music box ran down.

It did not go well at first. It went very badly. On the second night he trod on her so hard that she had to sit down on a bench and take her shoe off, and he knelt on the floor in front of her looking so miserable and apologetic that she found herself saying "It's fine, it's fine, stop looking like a kicked Crup," and patting his hair, which she hadn't meant to do and which startled them both so much they didn't speak for a whole waltz.

On the third night he got through one entire song without stepping on her at all, and was so pleased with himself that he immediately fell over a bench.

On the fourth night, she stopped counting out loud.

On the fifth night, she realized halfway through the second waltz that she'd forgotten to correct him for nearly two full minutes, because he'd stopped needing it, and because she'd been looking at the torchlight catching in his glasses and thinking about nothing at all.

"You're not counting," said Harry.

"You don't need me to."

"Oh." He looked down at their feet in astonishment, as if they belonged to somebody else. "I don't, do I?"

"Don't look at them. You'll fall over again."

He looked back up. At her. They were very close. Closer, somehow, than they had been on the first night, though they were standing exactly the same way: his hand between her shoulder blades, her hand in his, the tinny little music box tinkling away on the nearest glass case.

"You're good at this," said Harry quietly.

"I've had lessons since I was six." Her voice came out strange. "My mother insisted. Every Malfoy girl learns."

"No, I mean..." He hesitated. "You're good at teaching. You're patient."

Nobody had ever called Draconia Malfoy patient in her life.

"I am not patient," she said. "I've been secretly planning your murder for five days."

He grinned. "You patted my hair."

"That was an assault."

On the sixth night, at the end of the last waltz, he spun her.

She hadn't taught him that. She hadn't even mentioned it. He just did it: lifted their joined hands, and turned her under his arm, one full, smooth, perfect circle, so that her hair flew out and the torches blurred gold around her, and she came back to face him laughing out loud before she could stop herself.

The music box ran down. The last note hung in the air.

Harry was looking at her as though he'd never heard her laugh before. She supposed he hadn't. Not like that.

"Where did you learn that?" she said, breathless.

"I watched Fred and Angelina in the common room." His ears had gone pink. "Was it all right?"

Draconia smoothed down her hair, slowly. She took a step back. She lifted her chin.

"Adequate," she said.

Harry laughed so hard he had to sit down.

✦ ✦ ✦

By the seventh day, the whole school knew.

She never found out how. It might have been Pansy, though Pansy swore on her grandmother's grave that it wasn't. It might have been one of the portraits in the trophy room corridor; portraits were terrible gossips. It might simply have been that Harry Potter was a Triwizard champion, and everybody wanted to know who he was taking, and somebody had seen something.

Either way, by Tuesday morning, the question was being whispered in every corridor in the castle, in tones ranging from astonishment to horror.

Potter's taking MALFOY?

The Gryffindors looked at her as if she were a dragon that had wandered into their common room. The Hufflepuffs whispered. The Ravenclaws speculated. And the Slytherins...

The Slytherins were quiet. That was worse.

"Blood traitor," somebody murmured, very softly, as she passed the sixth-years' table at breakfast. She didn't turn round. She didn't have to. Greta, walking behind her, stopped very deliberately beside the sixth-years' table and stood there, looming, saying nothing at all, until the boy who'd said it looked down at his plate and stayed there.

Then Greta caught up with her, and they walked on.

"Thank you," said Draconia quietly, without looking at her.

Greta shrugged her large shoulders. "You'll look nice," she said. "In your dress."

It was the longest speech Draconia had ever heard her make.

She hadn't told her parents. She sat down that night to write the letter, and dipped her quill, and wrote Dear Mother and Father, and then sat looking at the page for an hour, and wrote nothing else.

Her father would find out eventually. Her father always did.

But not before Christmas. Not before the ball. That much, at least, she could have.

✦ ✦ ✦

Christmas night, Pansy did her hair.

"Down," Pansy said firmly, when Draconia reached for the pins. "I told you. You look best with it down. Hold still."

And so she wore it down: long and golden and shining, all the way past her shoulders, with just two thin braids at the front woven back and pinned behind her head with tiny emerald clips that Pansy had borrowed from someone, so that it looked like a crown that had grown there on its own.

Her gold hoops. Polished.

The pale gold silk, with the long flowing sleeves and the little emeralds at the neckline, catching every light in the room and turning it warm.

When she stood up in front of the tall mirror by the door, the whole dormitory went silent.

"Oh, Draconia," said Millicent.

Draconia looked at the girl in the mirror. Pale, still. Pointed chin, still. Straight golden hair that had never done anything interesting in its life.

But for once, she didn't look like a ghost with good posture.

For once, she looked like someone who might be worth asking properly.

"He's going to fall over," said Pansy, with deep satisfaction. "He's going to walk into a pillar."

✦ ✦ ✦

He didn't walk into a pillar. But it was close.

The champions and their partners were to wait in a little chamber off the entrance hall, and then walk into the Great Hall together to open the ball. Draconia came up the stairs from the dungeons at exactly five minutes to eight, holding her skirts in one hand, with Pansy and Blaise Zabini trailing behind her.

The entrance hall was packed with students in dress robes, a sea of colors and glitter and nerves. And at the foot of the marble staircase stood Harry.

He was wearing dress robes of bottle green.

Not scarlet. Not Gryffindor gold. Green. A deep, dark, glowing green, like the Slytherin banners, like the lake at night, like his own eyes. Like hers.

She stopped on the last stair.

He turned round, and saw her, and his mouth actually fell open.

For a long moment, he didn't say anything. He didn't seem to be able to. People were starting to look. Somebody giggled.

"You look..." said Harry at last, and stopped.

Draconia's heart was pounding so hard she could feel it in her ears. She came down the last stair, and stood in front of him, and lifted her chin.

"Say it properly, Potter."

He swallowed.

"Beautiful," said Harry. "You look beautiful."

And Draconia Malfoy, who had spent nearly two weeks rehearsing exactly how coolly she would receive any compliment tonight, opened her mouth to say adequate, and couldn't, and went so pink that it clashed with her dress.

"Your robes are green," she managed.

"Mrs. Weasley picked them." He looked down at himself, a little helplessly. "She said they'd go with my eyes."

"They do," said Draconia, before she could stop it. And then, very fast: "They go with my eyes too. People will think we planned it."

Harry looked at her eyes. Then at his robes. Then back at her eyes.

"Huh," he said slowly. "They're the same color."

"They are not."

"They are. They're exactly the..."

"Potter."

He grinned. Then he offered her his arm, the way she'd shown him on the fourth night, crooked just so, and she put her hand on it, and the whole entrance hall watched them walk together to the champions' chamber.

✦ ✦ ✦

That was when she saw Hermione.

She almost didn't recognize her. Hermione Granger, who had worn the same untidy school robes and the same bushy, frizzy mass of hair every day for four years, came down the marble staircase in robes of floating periwinkle blue, with her hair made smooth and shining and twisted up in an elegant knot at the back of her head. She was on the arm of Viktor Krum, the Bulgarian Seeker, the most famous young Quidditch player in the world.

The entrance hall gasped. Ron Weasley, somewhere by the doors, looked as though he'd been hit by a Bludger.

Hermione walked past them to the champions' chamber, holding her head high, with a little nervous smile, and her eyes flicked to Draconia's face, just for a moment, and away again. Bracing herself.

For me, Draconia realized. She's bracing herself for what I'm going to say.

She thought about a girl kneeling in the grass on a bright morning, asking what a word meant. She thought about a library table covered in books about hippogriff law, and a line of ink across a page, and a slap that had knocked her earring into the grass.

"Granger," she said.

Hermione stopped. Her shoulders tightened. Krum looked down at her, frowning slightly.

"You look lovely," said Draconia. Her voice came out stiff and awkward and far too formal. "Truly. The blue suits you."

Hermione stared at her.

It was a long stare. Draconia made herself stand still under it.

"Thank you," said Hermione at last, slowly, as if she was testing the words for traps. And then, even more slowly: "So do you. The gold." A pause. "Malfoy."

Then Krum was leading her on, and Harry was looking at Draconia with an expression she didn't know how to read, and McGonagall was calling the champions into line.

"What?" said Draconia, a little defensively.

"Nothing," said Harry. He was smiling, though. Not the grin. The other smile. The small, soft one from the lake. "Nothing at all."

✦ ✦ ✦

The Great Hall had been transformed.

The walls were covered in sparkling silver frost. Hundreds of garlands of mistletoe and ivy crossed the starry black ceiling. The house tables were gone; instead there were a hundred small, lantern-lit tables, and at the top of the room, a space had been cleared for dancing, with the band waiting on a raised platform.

Everyone stood and applauded as the champions walked in. Draconia kept her chin up and her face perfectly calm and her hand on Harry's arm, and felt every single eye in the room on her like sunlight.

And then the band struck up the first slow note, and the champions walked out onto the empty floor to open the ball.

Harry's arm was trembling under her hand.

"I can't do this," he muttered. "Everybody's looking. I'm going to step on you. I'm going to step on you in front of the entire school."

"You won't." She turned to face him. She took his right hand and put it on her back, just between her shoulder blades. She laced the fingers of her other hand through his. "Look at me. Not at them. Not at your feet. At me."

He looked at her.

"One," she whispered, "two, three."

And they danced.

He didn't step on her. Not once. He was stiff for the first few bars, and then, slowly, he forgot the whole school was watching, and forgot to be stiff, and forgot about his feet altogether, and simply danced, the way he'd danced on the sixth night in the trophy room: carefully, and then less carefully, and then with a kind of quiet joy.

Around them, the other champions turned in the candlelight. Fleur, silver and lovely, with a handsome Ravenclaw. Krum, with Hermione glowing in blue. And Cedric Diggory, with Cho Chang in his arms.

Draconia saw Cho over Harry's shoulder, and braced herself, and watched Harry's face.

He didn't look at Cho once.

Not once, the entire dance. She watched for it. She waited for his eyes to drift toward that shining dark hair, and they didn't. They stayed on her face, the whole time, as if there were nothing else in the Great Hall worth looking at.

Something inside her chest unknotted that had been tied for months.

And near the end of the waltz, as the music swelled, Harry lifted their joined hands high, and spun her.

The Great Hall turned around her in a blur of silver frost and candlelight and gold, and her hair flew out around her, and she came back to face him, and she was laughing. Openly, helplessly, in front of the entire school, in a way she had never once laughed at Hogwarts in her life.

The applause, when the music ended, was deafening.

Draconia didn't hear it. She was standing in the middle of the dance floor with her hand still in Harry's, both of them out of breath, looking at each other.

"Well?" said Harry.

"Adequate," she said, but she couldn't stop smiling, and he knew it, and she knew he knew it, and neither of them cared.

✦ ✦ ✦

Later, when the hall had grown hot and loud and Ron Weasley had spent a full hour glaring across the room at Viktor Krum, they slipped outside.

The front doors had been opened onto the grounds, and the lawns had been turned into a rose garden full of winding paths and fairy lights and stone benches. It was cold, and very quiet after the Great Hall, and the stars were brilliant overhead.

They walked in silence for a while. It wasn't an uncomfortable silence. It was the kind you could rest in.

Then, on the other side of a tall hedge, two voices.

"...no reason whatsoever to panic, Igor."

Snape's voice. Low and cold.

"Panic? Severus, you feel it as well as I do." It was Professor Karkaroff, the headmaster of Durmstrang, and he sounded frightened. "It grows darker every week. Clearer. Tell me you haven't noticed..."

"If you are so afraid," said Snape's voice, "then leave. I shan't stop you."

The voices moved away. Draconia had gone very still.

Darker every week. Clearer.

She knew what they meant. She knew, all at once and with total certainty, what was getting clearer on a grown man's forearm, and she knew it because she'd seen it once, at six years old, faded and grey, just before her father pulled his sleeve down very fast.

Harry was looking at her. "What is it? What's wrong?"

"Nothing."

"You've gone white."

She sat down on the nearest stone bench, because her legs had stopped working, and pulled her arms around herself. The silk was far too thin for December.

Harry sat down beside her. He didn't say anything. He just waited.

So she told him.

About the dressing room, and the sleeve, and the mark. About the campsite, and how she'd known her father's walk under the mask. About the letter she'd read three times, looking for something that wasn't there.

She'd never told anybody. Not Pansy. Not her mother. Not even herself, really, not in words, not out loud.

Harry listened to all of it. He didn't interrupt. He didn't look disgusted. He didn't say I knew it or of course he is or any of the things she'd been so sure he would say.

When she'd finished, he was quiet for a long moment.

Then he took off his outer robe, the bottle-green one, and put it round her shoulders.

"You're cold," he said.

"I'm not."

"You're shaking."

"That's..." She stopped. She pulled the robe tighter. It was warm from him, and smelled of broom polish and candle smoke. "That's something else."

They sat together on the bench under the fairy lights, and he didn't let go of her hand, and she didn't ask him to.

"It's not you," Harry said at last. "Whatever he is. Whatever that mark is. It's not you."

"You don't know that."

"I do," said Harry. "I watched you tell Hermione she looked lovely tonight."

Draconia looked at him. His face was very close. The fairy lights were catching in his glasses. Somewhere very far away, the band was still playing.

He leaned in, just slightly. So did she. She didn't decide to. It simply happened, the way her hand had grabbed his broom's tail once, without asking her first...

A rosebush exploded ten feet away.

It went up in a burst of petals and sparks, and a sixth-year couple who had been hiding behind it shrieked and fled, and Snape's voice snarled out of the darkness: "Twenty points from Hufflepuff! Back inside, the pair of you!"

Draconia and Harry sprang apart.

For one frozen second, they stared at each other.

Then they both ran, hand in hand, down the winding path between the hedges, with Snape's footsteps coming after them, and they didn't stop until they'd reached the front steps of the castle, and collapsed against the stone doorframe, gasping and laughing so hard they couldn't breathe.

✦ ✦ ✦

He walked her to the top of the dungeon stairs at midnight.

The torches were burning low. The music from the Great Hall had faded to a distant hum. She was still wearing his green robe. She'd forgotten to give it back, and he hadn't asked.

They stopped at the top step.

"Thank you," said Draconia, "for asking properly."

"Thanks for saying yes." He grinned. "Eventually."

She took off his robe, slowly, and held it out to him. He took it. Their fingers touched on the green velvet, and stayed there a moment too long.

And then Draconia Malfoy, who had never in her life done anything without planning it first, rose up onto her toes and kissed Harry Potter on the cheek.

It was very quick. Barely a brush. She felt the warmth of his skin, and the edge of his glasses frame against her temple, and then she was stepping back, and turning, and walking away down the dungeon stairs very fast, holding up her golden skirts, before he could say anything at all.

She didn't look back.

But at the bottom of the stairs, in the shadows, she stopped and pressed her back against the cold stone wall, and put both hands over her face, and stood there in the dark with her heart going like a hummingbird.

Above her, at the top of the stairs, she heard absolutely nothing for a very long time.

Then she heard Harry Potter say, softly, to nobody at all: "Wow."

✦ ✦ ✦

She didn't know, that night, that someone had taken a photograph.

She didn't know that a photographer from the Daily Prophet had been at the Yule Ball, sent by a journalist named Rita Skeeter, and that he had caught the exact moment on the dance floor when Harry Potter lifted his hand high and spun the heiress of the Malfoy family, laughing, under the silver frost.

She didn't know that the photograph would be on the front page on the twenty-seventh of December.

Or that three days later, a great eagle owl would arrive at the Slytherin table with a letter from her father.

It was one line long.

It was not we'll see.

Chapter Ten: The Losing Side

The letter said:

You will not speak to the Potter boy again.

That was all. No greeting. No signature. Just her father's handwriting, sharp and black and slanting, and underneath it, folded into the envelope, the front page of the Daily Prophet from the twenty-seventh of December, with the photograph: Harry lifting her hand high, and her turning beneath it in a whirl of pale gold silk, laughing.

In the photograph, she kept spinning, over and over, laughing every time.

She sat at the Slytherin breakfast table and looked at it for a long time. Then she folded the newspaper very small, and put it inside her Transfiguration book with the other two notes, and put the letter in the fire.

Her father had never, once in her whole life, told her no.

He'd just done it now, in one line.

✦ ✦ ✦

She obeyed him.

She didn't know what else to do. She had been Lucius Malfoy's daughter for fourteen years and Harry Potter's dance partner for one night, and when it came to it, the fourteen years won. They had to. They were the ground she stood on.

So when Harry came up to her in the entrance hall on the first morning of term, grinning, with his hands in his pockets, and said "Hi," she looked straight through him and walked past.

She didn't look back. She didn't have to. She could feel his face change behind her, the way you can feel a door close in another room.

She did it again at lunch. She did it again the next day, and the next. When he tried to catch her eye across the Great Hall, she looked at her plate. When he passed her in a corridor and slowed down, she speeded up. When he left a note in her Transfiguration book, Did I do something?, she tore it in half without answering and then, at midnight, sat up in bed and pieced the two halves together again and put them under her pillow.

"You're being horrible," said Pansy, in the dormitory, after the second week. "He looks like a kicked Crup. What happened?"

"Nothing happened."

"Draconia."

"My father happened," said Draconia, and pulled her curtains shut, and did not open them again until morning.

✦ ✦ ✦

The second task was in February.

The whole school gathered on the banks of the Black Lake to watch. Nobody knew what the task was, except the champions, and all anybody could get out of them was that it involved the water, and that it involved getting something back.

Draconia never saw the lake that morning.

The night before, Professor McGonagall had come down to the Slytherin common room and asked, very quietly, whether Miss Malfoy would come with her to the headmaster's office. And Draconia had followed her up through the castle in her dressing gown, bewildered, and found Hermione Granger waiting outside the stone gargoyle, and a small silvery-haired girl who must have been Fleur Delacour's little sister, and Cho Chang.

She'd looked at the four of them. And at Dumbledore's grave, kind face when he explained.

Each champion will have one hour to recover what was taken from them. Something they will sorely miss.

Hermione, for Krum. Cho, for Cedric. Gabrielle, for her sister.

And her. For Harry.

"There must be some mistake," Draconia had said, in a small, strange voice. "Professor. He wouldn't choose... He hasn't spoken to me in six weeks. I've been..."

"The lake does not choose, Miss Malfoy," said Dumbledore gently, "and neither does the champion. We simply asked ourselves which of his treasures Harry would miss the most." His blue eyes twinkled behind his half-moon spectacles. "It was not, I assure you, a difficult question."

Then he'd raised his wand, and the world went soft and grey and warm, and she slept.

✦ ✦ ✦

She woke up in the lake.

It was the cold that woke her first, sudden and savage, a thousand needles all over her skin. Then the light, grey and shocking. Then the noise: a vast roar from somewhere above her, a crowd screaming.

She was being held. Someone had an arm locked around her from behind, hauling her up through the water, and she broke the surface coughing and choking, and gasped in a great ragged lungful of air.

"I've got you," said a voice, right by her ear. "I've got you. You're all right. I've got you."

She knew that voice.

She twisted round in the water. Harry's face was right there, white with cold, his lips blue, his hair plastered flat. There was something strange about his neck: gills, she realized dimly, fading, closing up. He had one arm around her and the other around the little silver-haired girl, Gabrielle, who was sobbing and spluttering on his other side.

"You took two," said Draconia, through chattering teeth.

"She was going to be left down there." Harry was gasping. "Fleur didn't come. I couldn't just..."

"You're an idiot," said Draconia, and burst into tears.

She didn't even know she was going to. It just came out of her, there in the freezing water, with the whole school screaming on the bank: great, choking, hiccuping sobs. Because she'd been sleeping at the bottom of a lake, and she'd been his treasure, the thing he would miss the most. After six weeks of her walking past him in corridors like he didn't exist. After she'd torn his note in half.

He'd still come to get her.

"Hey," said Harry, alarmed. "Hey, don't... I've got you. We're nearly there. Kick. Can you kick?"

She kicked. They swam. The bank came closer, and hands reached down, Madam Pomfrey's and Percy Weasley's and a dozen others, and hauled all three of them up out of the water onto the grass, and wrapped them in thick blankets, and poured scalding potion down their throats until steam came out of their ears.

Draconia sat on the bank, shaking, wrapped in a blanket to her chin, and looked up.

The whole school was looking at her.

At her. Every face in the stands. Slytherin, Gryffindor, Ravenclaw, Hufflepuff. They'd all seen what Harry Potter had gone to the bottom of the lake to bring back.

It hadn't been Ron Weasley. It hadn't been Cho Chang.

It had been Draconia Malfoy.

She thought about the Daily Prophet. She thought about the photographer, who was almost certainly here. She thought about her father's letter, burning in the common room fire.

You will not speak to the Potter boy again.

She looked at Harry, sitting beside her in his own blanket, his lips still blue, watching her anxiously.

"Why?" she whispered. "I've been awful. For six weeks. Why would you still...?"

"Because you were awful," said Harry, "for six weeks. And you weren't, for three and a half years before that." His teeth were chattering. "I figured something happened."

She held his gaze. She didn't look away this time.

"My father saw the photograph," she said.

He was quiet. Then: "Oh."

"He told me not to speak to you. Ever again."

"Oh," said Harry again, more slowly.

"I'm speaking to you now," said Draconia. "In front of the entire school."

Harry looked around at the stands. At the hundreds of watching faces. At the photographer from the Prophet, who was, indeed, pushing his way along the bank toward them with his camera raised.

"Yeah," he said. "You are."

"So," said Draconia, with a slightly hysterical laugh, "I suppose I'm in rather a lot of trouble."

And he took her cold hand under the blankets and held it, and didn't let go, not even when the camera flashed.

Further along the bank, wrapped in a blanket of her own, with Viktor Krum hovering over her like an anxious bear, Hermione Granger was watching them.

When Harry was finally called away by the judges, Hermione got up, and walked over, and sat down beside Draconia on the wet grass without being asked.

They sat there for a while. Both of them dripping. Both of them shaking.

"Krum's hostage and Harry's hostage," said Hermione at last, through chattering teeth. "Next to each other. On a riverbank. Who'd have thought."

"Not me," said Draconia.

Another silence. The crowd was still roaring. Somewhere, Ludo Bagman was announcing scores.

"Granger," said Draconia. She stared straight ahead at the grey water. Her voice came out rough. "In second year. On the Quidditch pitch. That word I called you..."

Hermione went very still.

"I..." Draconia started, and stopped. The words were right there. She could feel their shape. Three of them, very simple, that she'd never once said to anybody in her life.

"Hermione!" Ron Weasley came pelting along the bank, red in the face, and flung a second blanket over Hermione's head, and started asking whether she was all right and what that great Bulgarian lump thought he was doing letting her get so cold, and the moment was gone.

Hermione let Ron drag her away. But at the top of the bank, she looked back, just once, at Draconia sitting alone on the grass.

It was a long look. A thoughtful one.

As if she'd heard the words anyway.

✦ ✦ ✦

After that, they met in secret.

It was the only way. She wrote to her father and said all the right things: that it had been Dumbledore's choice, not hers, that the photograph meant nothing, that she had not spoken to the Potter boy since, and would not. Her father's reply was cold and short and suspicious, and she read it, and burned it, and at eight o'clock that night went to the trophy room.

Harry was already there. He always was.

They didn't dance, most nights. They talked. About Quidditch, and lessons, and the golden egg that screamed when you opened it, and the third task. About her father. About his aunt and uncle, and the cupboard under the stairs where he'd slept until he was eleven, which he told her about one night in a flat, quiet voice while she sat very still beside him and felt something in her chest turn hot and furious on behalf of a small boy she'd never met.

She told him about the Manor. The peacocks. The tutors. A Malfoy never asks for anything. A Malfoy is offered things.

"That's stupid," said Harry.

"It's not stupid, it's..." She stopped. "It is a bit stupid."

"You asked me to ask you properly," said Harry. "That was asking for something."

Draconia opened her mouth, and closed it, and looked at him in the torchlight with his untidy hair and his crooked glasses, and thought: Yes. I suppose it was.

✦ ✦ ✦

The third task was on the twenty-fourth of June.

It was a maze. The Quidditch pitch had been grown over with enormous hedges, twenty feet high, dark and dense, and somewhere at the center of them sat the Triwizard Cup. The first champion to touch it would win.

She found Harry that afternoon, before the task, in the empty corridor behind the Great Hall.

He looked pale and determined and very young. He had his wand in one hand. He kept turning it over and over.

She didn't say anything. She'd been thinking about it all day, what to say, and she'd decided in the end that there wasn't anything. So instead, she reached up and untied the thin green silk ribbon from her hair. The one Moody had knocked out, the day she was a ferret. The one Harry had knelt down and handed back to her, without a word.

She took his right wrist, and tied the ribbon around it. A neat double knot. She pulled it tight.

"For luck," she said. "Don't lose it. It's silk."

Harry looked down at the green ribbon on his wrist. Then up at her.

"I'll bring it back," he said.

"You'd better."

"I will."

"Then go and win, Potter," she said, and her voice only shook a little. "And if you get eaten by something in that maze, I will never, ever forgive you."

He grinned. It was a small grin, and a scared one. But it was real.

Then he was gone, down the corridor, toward the pitch.

✦ ✦ ✦

She sat in the stands for two hours while the sky went dark.

You couldn't see anything, once the champions went into the maze. Just the tall black hedges and, every so often, a burst of red sparks shooting up from somewhere inside, which meant somebody was in trouble and a teacher had to go in and get them out. Fleur's sparks went up. Then Krum's. And the crowd murmured and craned their necks and waited.

Harry's didn't.

Neither did Cedric's.

The waiting went on and on. The stars came out. Draconia sat with her hands clenched together in her lap so tightly her gold hoops trembled, and didn't speak to Pansy, and didn't speak to anyone.

And then there was a flash of light on the grass in front of the maze.

A figure appeared, out of nowhere, sprawled on the ground. Two figures. The band started to play. The crowd began to cheer, and stand, and scream with joy...

And then stopped.

Because one of the figures was Harry, and he was holding onto the other one. Holding onto him with both hands, face down in the grass, his whole body shaking. And the other figure wasn't moving.

It was Cedric Diggory. And Cedric's eyes were open, and blank, and staring at the sky.

Somebody screamed. Somebody far away said the word dead, and then it was everywhere, rushing through the stands like wind through grass, dead, he's dead, Cedric Diggory's dead...

Draconia was on her feet.

She was pushing past people. She didn't care who they were. She was going down the stand steps two at a time, like the day in the storm, the day he'd fallen, and nobody stopped her, because nobody was looking at her. They were all looking at the boy on the grass, holding on to the dead one, refusing to let go.

She got close enough to hear him.

He was saying something, over and over, to Dumbledore, to anyone. His voice was cracked and broken and wrong.

"He's back. He's back. Voldemort's back."

Draconia stopped dead at the edge of the grass.

The name went through her like cold water. She had never heard anyone say it aloud before. Nobody in her family said it. Her father said the Dark Lord, in that quiet careful voice, and only when he thought nobody was listening.

He's back.

She thought about a dressing room. A sleeve, rolled down too fast. A mark, faded and grey, that had been growing darker all year.

She thought: Father will be so pleased.

And she was sick, right there on the edge of the Quidditch pitch, in front of everyone.

✦ ✦ ✦

She saw him three days later.

He'd been in the hospital wing, and then shut away in Dumbledore's office, and the Gryffindors guarded him like a pack of dogs, and she couldn't get anywhere near. So she waited. She went to the Owlery every evening at eight o'clock, because it was the only place in the castle where nobody ever came, and she hoped.

On the third evening, he came up the stairs.

He looked terrible. Thinner. Grey under the eyes. There was a half-healed cut on his arm, and he walked like someone who hadn't slept since the maze.

He stopped at the top of the stairs. She stood up.

For a moment, neither of them said anything.

Then he held out his right wrist.

The green silk ribbon was still tied around it, in her neat double knot. It was filthy. It was torn at one end, and stained with mud and grass and something darker.

"I said I'd bring it back," Harry said.

His voice broke on the last word.

Draconia crossed the Owlery in three steps and put her arms around him.

She didn't think about it. She didn't decide. She just did it, and held on, and felt him go very still, and then felt his arms come up around her, and his face press into her shoulder, and his whole body start to shake.

She didn't say anything. She didn't say it's all right, because it wasn't. She didn't say it's not your fault, because he wouldn't believe her. She just held on, in the dark, among the sleeping owls, for a very long time.

When he finally let go, he wiped his face on his sleeve, roughly, and didn't look at her.

"There's something you should know," he said. "About the graveyard."

She knew. She'd known since the edge of the Quidditch pitch. But she made herself say it anyway.

"My father was there."

Harry looked up at her. His green eyes were red-rimmed and very tired.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "He was."

The owls rustled overhead.

"He was wearing a mask," said Harry. "But Voldemort called them all by name. He called your dad by name." He swallowed. "He was the first one to kneel."

Draconia sat down on the cold stone floor, all at once, because her legs had simply stopped holding her up.

Harry sat down next to her.

After a long time, she said: "What am I supposed to do?"

"I don't know," said Harry.

It was the most honest answer anyone had ever given her.

✦ ✦ ✦

On the last night of term, there was no end-of-year feast. There was a memorial.

The Great Hall was hung in black. Nobody spoke much. At the end of the meal, Dumbledore stood, and told the whole school the truth: that Cedric Diggory had been murdered, and that Lord Voldemort had returned. The Ministry, he said, did not wish him to tell them. He was telling them anyway.

Then he raised his goblet to Cedric.

All across the Great Hall, benches scraped as people got to their feet. The Hufflepuffs, weeping. The Ravenclaws. The Gryffindors. Even the students from Durmstrang and Beauxbatons.

At the Slytherin table, nobody moved.

Draconia looked along the table. At Vivienne, sitting with her arms folded. At Greta, looking at her plate. At Pansy, halfway up, looking round anxiously to see what everyone else was doing, and sinking back down. At the sixth-years, stone-faced, not one of them rising.

She thought about Cedric Diggory, who had been frightfully decent, and a Hufflepuff, and had waltzed past her with Cho Chang at the Yule Ball and smiled at her kindly as he passed.

She stood up.

It was very quiet at the Slytherin end. She felt every eye on her back. She lifted her goblet anyway, and held it steady, and said "Cedric Diggory," with the rest of the Hall.

Across the room, over the rows of standing heads, Harry Potter was looking at her.

He didn't smile. It wasn't a night for smiling. But he lifted his goblet, very slightly, toward her. And she lifted hers back.

When she sat down again, Greta was on her feet beside her. Late, and awkward, and alone, but standing.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Hogwarts Express left for London on the last day of term, and she rode it in the front compartment, with Pansy and Vivienne and Greta, the way she always had.

Nobody talked much. The whole train was quiet. Cedric's death sat over everything like a fog.

Halfway to London, Draconia went out into the corridor. She didn't know why. She just needed to breathe.

And the door of a compartment slid open further down, and Harry came out, with Ron and Hermione behind him.

They stopped.

Behind her, the door of her own compartment was still open. Vivienne and Greta were watching. And Draconia remembered, with a cold, sudden clarity, whose daughters Vivienne and Greta were. Remembered what Harry had said in the Owlery: He called them all by name. Remembered that Mr. Crabbe and Mr. Goyle had knelt in that graveyard too.

Everything she said now would go home. Every single word.

She lifted her chin.

"You've picked the losing side, Potter," she said. Clearly. Loudly enough to carry. Her voice was steady and cold and perfect, and it was her father's voice, and she hated it. "I told you. On this train. Our very first day. I told you which company was better. You should have listened."

Ron's face went scarlet. He started forward, his hand going to his wand, and Hermione grabbed his arm.

Harry didn't move.

He just looked at her, down the length of the swaying corridor. And she looked back, and put everything she couldn't say into her face: I'm sorry. They're listening. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.

She saw him see it.

"I know which side I'm on," Harry said quietly. "Do you?"

Then he turned, and went back into his compartment, and slid the door shut.

Draconia stood in the corridor for a long time, with the train rattling beneath her feet, and her eyes stinging, and the green hills rushing past the windows.

When she went back into her compartment, Vivienne looked up at her with a small, satisfied smile.

"Good," said Vivienne. "Your father will be pleased."

"Yes," said Draconia, and sat down, and looked out of the window, and didn't say another word until London.

✦ ✦ ✦

King's Cross was crowded and bright and loud. Her father was waiting on the platform, tall and pale in his black cloak, with his silver-headed cane. He saw her, and smiled his thin smile, and put his left hand on her shoulder.

It was heavy. It had always been heavy. She'd never noticed before.

She looked back once, over her shoulder, through the crowd.

Harry was standing by the barrier with the Weasleys, and he was looking at her. He didn't wave. He didn't smile. He just lifted his right hand, very slightly, so that she could see it.

The green silk ribbon was still tied around his wrist.

He'd never given it back.

Draconia turned away, and walked out of King's Cross with her father's hand on her shoulder, and she did not cry.

She would have plenty of time for that over the summer.

Chapter Eleven: Not Invited

The summer was very quiet at Malfoy Manor, except at night.

At night, there were visitors.

Draconia heard them arrive long after she was supposed to be asleep. The crack of Apparition on the gravel drive. Low voices in the entrance hall. Footsteps going down, and down, to the rooms below the Manor that she had never been allowed to enter. Once she crept out onto the landing in her nightdress and looked over the banister, and saw three men in black cloaks follow her father across the hall. One of them was Mr. Crabbe. One of them was Mr. Goyle.

The third one turned his head and looked up, straight at her, as if he had known all along that she was there.

She didn't know his face. She never wanted to see it again.

She went back to bed and lay there with the covers pulled up to her chin, and she did not creep out onto the landing again.

✦ ✦ ✦

In the daytime, her father read the Daily Prophet at breakfast, and smiled.

The Prophet had decided, that summer, that Harry Potter was a liar. It didn't say so outright. It was cleverer than that. It said things like a troubled boy and a disturbed young man and a boy who has always craved attention. It printed little jokes about him in the middle of other stories. It said that Dumbledore was going senile. It said that there was no evidence whatsoever that You-Know-Who had returned, and that anybody who said otherwise was either lying or mad.

Every morning her father read it, and smiled his thin smile, and passed it across to her mother.

In August, there was a new story. Harry Potter had performed underage magic in front of a Muggle. He claimed it had been in self-defence, against two Dementors, in the middle of a Muggle town. Dementors in Little Whinging, the Prophet sneered. The boy truly will say anything. There was to be a full criminal trial at the Ministry of Magic. He would almost certainly be expelled from Hogwarts.

Draconia read the story three times. Her toast went cold.

Two Dementors. She thought about the train in third year. The frost on the windows. The sound in her head. She thought about Harry, alone, in some Muggle street, with those things coming at him out of the dark.

"Excellent," said her father, reading over her shoulder. "Expelled, and his wand snapped. One less problem."

Draconia dropped her fork. It hit her plate with a sharp, bright clang that rang through the whole silent dining room.

Her father looked at her.

"I'm sorry, Father," she said. "It slipped."

He looked at her for a moment longer than he needed to. Then he went back to the newspaper.

Two days later, the Prophet reported, very small, on page seven, that Harry Potter had been cleared of all charges.

Draconia read that story in her bedroom with the door locked, and then lay face down on her bed and cried into her pillow for ten minutes, and never knew exactly why.

✦ ✦ ✦

On the last night of the summer, her father called her into his study.

It was a dark, beautiful room, lined with books and portraits, with a fire even in August. He was sitting behind his desk. He did not ask her to sit down.

"You are fifteen," he said.

"Yes, Father."

"In two years, you will come of age." He turned his wand over slowly between his long fingers. "It is time you began to think about your future, Draconia. About the family. About what is expected of the Malfoy heir."

She waited. Her heart was beating very fast, and she didn't know why.

"Great changes are coming," said her father softly. "The old ways. The true ways. There are those who value pure blood above all else, and who will reward families that have kept it pure." He looked at her. "Our blood is the oldest and the purest in Britain. It is not simply yours to spend as you please. Do you understand?"

"I..." She didn't. She didn't want to. "I think so, Father."

"Good." He smiled. It didn't reach his eyes. "Then there will be no more photographs in the Prophet."

She went up to bed and did not sleep at all.

✦ ✦ ✦

She was made a prefect that year.

The badge came in her Hogwarts letter, green and silver, with a large P on it, and her mother cried a little and kissed her forehead, and her father nodded as if it were no more than his due. Draconia pinned it on the front of her robes and looked at it in the mirror and felt nothing at all.

On the Hogwarts Express, all the prefects rode together in a special carriage at the front of the train.

She walked in with her chin up. And stopped.

Ron Weasley was sitting by the window, looking deeply embarrassed, with a prefect's badge on his jumper. And next to him, of course, was Hermione Granger, with her own badge polished so bright that it glinted.

"Oh," said Ron, when he saw her. "Brilliant."

"Weasley," said Draconia coolly. "Granger."

Hermione looked at her. It was a long, careful, measuring look, the same look she'd given her on the bank of the lake. Then she nodded, once.

"Malfoy," she said. Not unkindly.

It was more than Draconia had expected.

Harry wasn't there. Harry wasn't a prefect. She found herself wondering whether it had stung him, not being chosen, and then found herself furious on his behalf, and then found herself looking out of the window so that no one could see her face.

Later, walking the corridor on patrol, she passed a compartment near the back of the train and saw him.

He was sitting with Neville Longbottom, and Ginny Weasley, and a strange dreamy-looking girl with radishes hanging from her ears who was reading a magazine upside down. He looked thinner than he had in June. Older. There were shadows under his eyes that hadn't been there before.

He looked up and saw her through the glass.

For one moment, neither of them moved.

She saw his eyes go to her prefect's badge. She saw his right hand move, very slightly, on his knee. The green silk ribbon was still tied around his wrist.

Behind her, Vivienne and Greta were coming down the corridor. She could hear them.

She kept walking.

✦ ✦ ✦

The new Defence Against the Dark Arts teacher was a woman named Dolores Umbridge.

She was short and squat and wore fluffy pink cardigans, with a little black velvet bow in her curly hair, and she spoke in a high, girlish, breathy voice, like someone talking to a very small child. She came from the Ministry. She taught them nothing. She had them read a textbook, silently, for the entire lesson, every lesson, and when Hermione asked why they weren't going to practice any actual spells, she smiled her sweet toad-like smile and said that there was no need, because there was nothing out there to defend against.

"Nothing?" said Harry.

He hadn't raised his hand. The whole class went still.

"And what," said Umbridge sweetly, "do you imagine might be out there, Mr. Potter?"

"Voldemort," said Harry.

Draconia's quill stopped moving.

"He's back," said Harry. "I saw him. I was there. He killed Cedric Diggory."

"Detention, Mr. Potter," said Umbridge, still smiling. "Tomorrow evening. Five o'clock. My office."

She had never seen Harry look like he did then. So angry, and so absolutely certain, and so completely alone in a room full of people pretending not to hear him.

She looked down at her textbook.

She didn't say anything.

✦ ✦ ✦

She saw his hand a week later.

It was in the Owlery. She had started going there again at eight o'clock in the evenings, the way she had at the end of last year, without ever quite admitting to herself why. And on the Thursday, the door at the top of the tower stairs creaked open, and he was there.

He stopped when he saw her.

"Oh," he said. "It's you."

"It's the Owlery," said Draconia. "Anyone can come to the Owlery."

He didn't answer. He walked past her to the window, to where his snowy owl was sleeping, and stroked her feathers with his left hand. And Draconia saw the back of his right hand.

It was red, and raw, and swollen. And carved into the skin, in thin, fresh, bleeding lines, were words.

I must not tell lies.

She was across the Owlery before she knew she'd moved. She had his wrist in both her hands.

"What is this?"

He tried to pull away. She didn't let go.

"Potter. What is this?"

"Detention," said Harry flatly.

"Detention does this?"

"She has a quill. You write lines with it. It doesn't need ink." He looked at the window, not at her. "It cuts the words into the back of your hand while you write them. You keep going until it sinks in."

Draconia stared at the words on his hand. I must not tell lies. In his own handwriting. Cut into his own skin.

Something rose in her chest so hot and so violent that for a moment she couldn't see properly.

"That's illegal," she said. Her voice was shaking. "That's a Dark object. I know what that is. My father has one. It's locked in a cabinet in his study. It's illegal, Potter, you have to tell McGonagall, you have to tell Dumbledore..."

"And say what?" He pulled his hand away, finally. "That the Ministry's teacher is torturing me for saying Voldemort's back? Everyone already thinks I'm a lying, attention-seeking madman. You've read the Prophet. Your father reads the Prophet."

She flinched. She couldn't help it.

He saw. His face changed, just slightly.

She took a small glass bottle out of her pocket. She'd had it with her since the second day of term. She hadn't really known why.

"Murtlap essence," she said. "It helps. Soak your hand in it." She held it out. "I know you're not lying."

Harry looked at the bottle. Then at her.

"Then say it," he said quietly. "Not here. In the Great Hall. In front of Umbridge. In front of your house. Say you believe me."

The owls rustled overhead in the darkness.

Draconia stood there with the bottle held out in her hand, and opened her mouth, and closed it again.

She thought about the men in black cloaks on the gravel drive. About the one who had looked up and seen her on the landing. About Vivienne's father, and Greta's. About her own father, in his study, turning his wand over in his long fingers. Our blood is not yours to spend as you please.

"I can't," she said.

"No." Harry took the bottle from her hand. He didn't look angry anymore. He just looked tired. "I know you can't." He put it in his pocket. "Thanks for the murtlap."

He went down the stairs. She heard his footsteps go all the way down, and fade.

She stayed in the Owlery until the stars came out.

✦ ✦ ✦

After that, he started disappearing.

She noticed it the way she noticed everything about him: without meaning to, and all the time. He wasn't in the Owlery anymore. He wasn't in the library. On certain evenings, he simply vanished from the castle after dinner, and so did Ron, and so did Hermione, and so, she started to realize, did a great many other people.

Neville Longbottom. The Weasley twins. Ginny Weasley. Luna Lovegood, the girl with the radish earrings. A whole cluster of Hufflepuffs and a handful of Ravenclaws.

And Cho Chang.

Draconia saw it first on a Hogsmeade weekend in October. She'd been walking up the high street with Pansy, and she'd seen them, a whole group of them, sneaking one by one through the grimy door of the Hog's Head, the dirtiest, shadiest pub in the village. Harry had gone in first. Then Hermione, Ron, Neville, the twins, a dozen others.

And Cho, with a friend, glancing back over her shoulder to see if anyone was watching.

Then Cho went in too.

Draconia stood in the middle of the high street, in the cold October sunshine, and felt something small and cold settle into the pit of her stomach.

"Draconia?" said Pansy. "What are you staring at?"

"Nothing," said Draconia. "Let's go to Madam Puddifoot's."

She hated Madam Puddifoot's. It was pink, and frilly, and full of couples holding hands. She sat in it for an hour anyway, drinking tea she didn't taste, looking out of the window at the door of the Hog's Head across the street, until it opened again and they all came out, in ones and twos, looking pleased and secretive and excited about something.

Cho came out laughing, and touched Harry's arm as she passed him.

It was just a touch. Barely anything. Draconia saw it anyway.

✦ ✦ ✦

The next Saturday morning, very early, she went up to the Owlery.

She told herself it was to send a letter to her mother. She even had one with her, folded in her pocket, three lines long and entirely about the weather. It was barely light. The castle was still asleep. The frost was thick on the steps of the tower, and her breath smoked, and she climbed slowly, thinking that if he happened to be there, if he happened to have come to send a letter too, then that would be a coincidence, and nobody could possibly blame her for a coincidence.

She heard voices before she reached the top.

"...just thought I'd send it early," a girl was saying, a little breathlessly. "Before the rush. It's my mum's birthday on Monday."

"Right," said Harry's voice. "Yeah. Good idea."

Draconia stopped on the stairs, just below the doorway, in the shadow of the curve.

She could see them through the gap. Cho Chang, with a pink scarf wrapped around her neck and her dark hair loose over her shoulders, tying a parcel to the leg of a school owl. And Harry, standing a few feet away with his hands shoved in his pockets, looking at the floor, and then at Cho, and then at the floor again.

His ears were red.

"It's been really good," said Cho. "The... you know. The meetings. You're a really good teacher, Harry."

"Thanks," said Harry. His ears went redder.

"Cedric would have..." Cho stopped. Her voice wobbled. "Sorry. I don't know why I said that."

"No," said Harry quickly. "No, it's fine. He would have. He was..." He stopped too. "He was good."

They stood there for a moment, not quite looking at each other, in the cold grey light.

Teacher, thought Draconia. He's teaching them.

And then: He's teaching her.

She turned round on the stairs. She went back down, very quietly, all the way to the bottom, and stood in the frozen courtyard with the letter to her mother still in her pocket.

She didn't send it. She took it out and read it: three lines, all about the weather. Then she tore it into very small pieces, and let the wind take them across the frost.

A good teacher. Of course he was. He'd been teaching her to be braver for four years, and she'd never once said thank you.

✦ ✦ ✦

By November, she was sure.

Harry had some kind of club. A secret one. They met somewhere in the castle, she didn't know where, and they did something together that made them all walk around the next day with an air of pleased, private purpose. Neville Longbottom, who had never done a single successful spell in his life, cast a perfect Disarming Charm in Charms one morning and then looked terrified at himself.

And Cho Chang smiled at Harry now. Every day. Across the Great Hall. In corridors. On the stairs.

And sometimes, Harry smiled back.

Draconia sat at the Slytherin table and watched them do it, and ate her breakfast very slowly, and felt the old jealousy from fourth year come creeping back, colder and sharper than ever. Because it wasn't just Cho now. It was everything. All of them. A whole secret world of people who got to be with him, in the evenings, doing whatever it was they did, laughing about it the next morning.

And she was on the outside.

She hadn't been invited.

She told herself she didn't want to be. She told herself it was probably something stupid and Gryffindor and illegal and that she was well out of it. She told herself she couldn't have gone anyway, not with Vivienne and Greta watching her every move, not with her father's letters arriving every week, smooth and pleasant and full of questions about who she was spending her time with.

She told herself all of that.

It didn't help at all.

✦ ✦ ✦

She caught him alone, finally, in the second week of November.

He was coming out of the Charms corridor late, after everyone else had gone, and she stepped out of an alcove in front of him so suddenly that he nearly walked into her.

"You're doing something," she said.

He stopped. His face went very still and careful.

"I don't know what you..."

"Don't." She kept her voice low. There was no one else in the corridor, but she looked both ways anyway. "Don't lie to me, Potter. You're terrible at it. You go red at the ears." She took a breath. "You're doing something. With Weasley, and Granger, and Longbottom, and half of Gryffindor. And Chang. You all disappear after dinner and come back looking like you've won the Quidditch Cup. I'm not an idiot."

Harry didn't say anything.

"What is it?" she said. "Tell me."

"I can't."

The words landed like a slap. Worse than Hermione's, in a way. That one had been fair.

"Because I'm a Slytherin," she said.

"No." He shook his head. "Because your father knelt in a graveyard." His voice was very quiet. "And you still go home to him every summer. And you sit at breakfast with him, and he reads the Prophet, and it says I'm a liar, and you can't say it isn't true. Not even where Umbridge can't hear you."

She couldn't speak.

"It's not just me," said Harry. "If it was just me, I'd tell you. I'd tell you anything. But there are other people. Neville. Ginny. Luna. Their parents aren't... If anyone finds out, if it gets back to your father, or Crabbe's, or Goyle's..." He stopped. "I can't risk them. Not even for you."

Not even for you.

"I stood up," said Draconia. Her voice cracked right down the middle. "For Cedric. At the memorial. In front of everyone. I stood up."

"I know," said Harry. "I saw." For a moment, his face was so full of something she couldn't bear to look at it. "I'm sorry, Draconia."

It was the first time he had ever said her name.

Not Malfoy. Not ever Malfoy, not like that. Draconia.

And he was using it to say no.

He walked past her down the corridor. She didn't turn round to watch him go.

✦ ✦ ✦

She didn't go to the Owlery that night. Or the next night. Or the one after that.

Instead, she sat in her chair by the Slytherin common room fire every evening with a book open in her lap that she never read, and watched the flames, and thought about all the people who got to be in Harry Potter's secret. Neville. Luna. Ginny. Cho.

And not her.

Across the common room, Pansy was reading a notice that had just gone up on the board. Draconia didn't look at it. She didn't care what it said.

"Draconia," said Pansy. "Have you seen this?"

"No."

"You should." Pansy's voice was odd. Careful. "It's from Umbridge. She's starting something. A special squad of students. Prefects, mostly, from the right sort of families. To help her keep order." A pause. "They'll be allowed to take points. Give detentions. Search people." Another pause. "Find out about secret groups."

Draconia lifted her head.

She looked at the notice for a long time.

Then she got up, and crossed the common room, and read it for herself.

Pansy was watching her face.

"You wouldn't," said Pansy quietly. "Would you?"

Draconia didn't answer.

She was thinking about Cho Chang, laughing, touching Harry's arm on the steps of the Hog's Head. About a secret she hadn't been allowed into. About the way he'd said her name for the very first time, in an empty corridor, just to tell her no.

Not even for you.

She reached out, and touched the notice with one finger, very lightly.

Chapter Twelve: Manners, Potter

Professor Umbridge's office was pink.

Not a little pink. Entirely, overwhelmingly, suffocatingly pink. Pink lace doilies on every surface. Pink cushions. A pink tablecloth. And on the walls, dozens and dozens of ornamental china plates, each one painted with a fluffy kitten wearing a bow, and all of the kittens moving: mewing, frolicking, washing their paws, blinking their huge painted eyes at her.

Draconia stood in the middle of the carpet and tried not to breathe too deeply.

"Such a pleasure, Miss Malfoy," said Professor Umbridge, pouring tea from a pink china pot. "Do sit down. Sugar? No? How very disciplined. I would expect nothing less from your father's daughter."

"Thank you, Professor."

"A remarkable man, Lucius. A great friend to the Ministry. The Minister himself speaks very highly of him." Umbridge smiled her wide, flat, toad-like smile and pushed a small box across the desk. "And I have every confidence that his daughter will be a great asset to my little squad."

Draconia opened the box.

Inside, on a bed of pink velvet, lay a small silver badge in the shape of a letter I.

Inquisitorial Squad.

She picked it up. It was heavier than it looked.

"You will have the power to deduct house points," said Umbridge sweetly. "To report rule-breakers directly to me. To search students' bags, if you have reason to believe they are concealing something. I am particularly interested," she added, stirring her tea, "in any student who may be forming unauthorized groups."

Draconia pinned the silver I to her robes, just below her prefect's badge, and looked up at Professor Umbridge, and smiled.

It was her father's smile. Pleasant and cold. She'd been practising it in the mirror since she was six.

"I'll do my very best, Professor," she said.

✦ ✦ ✦

She told herself it was for her father.

His letters had been getting sharper all autumn. Who are you spending your time with? What is Potter doing? Mr. Crabbe tells me his daughter says you have been seen near the Owlery. The Inquisitorial Squad would stop the letters. It would make him proud. It would put her above suspicion, right where Vivienne and Greta could see her, doing exactly what a Malfoy was supposed to do.

That was what she told herself.

It was true, as far as it went.

But she also thought about the Hog's Head. And the Owlery steps in the frost. And Cho Chang, touching Harry's arm. You're a really good teacher. And a dark corridor, and Harry's voice, saying her name for the very first time: Not even for you.

The silver badge sat heavy on her chest.

She didn't take it off.

✦ ✦ ✦

She used it on him on the very first day.

He was coming round the corner of the Transfiguration corridor too fast, with his bag half open, late for something. He nearly ran straight into her. He stopped short, and his eyes went to the badge, and she saw his face change.

"You joined," he said.

"Good morning, Potter," said Draconia. "Walking too fast in a corridor. That's dangerous. You might knock someone over." She tilted her head. "Where are you going in such a hurry?"

"Nowhere."

"Nowhere is a long way away. You'll be late."

"What do you want, Malfoy?"

She lifted her chin.

"Manners, Potter," she said. "Or you'll be earning yourself a detention."

Harry stared at her. There was a long, charged, silent moment in the corridor.

"You wouldn't," he said.

"Try me."

He looked at her. And she looked back. And she saw, in his face, that he was angry, and hurt, and suspicious, and trying very hard to be all three at once, and failing at at least one of them.

"Right," said Harry at last. "Fine. Take your points, then."

"Very well." Draconia made a show of considering it. "One point from Gryffindor."

He blinked. "One?"

"For the hair," said Draconia. "It's a disgrace. It's been a disgrace for five years. Somebody had to do something about it."

For a moment, just for a moment, she saw the corner of his mouth twitch.

Then he got control of it, and scowled, and pushed past her down the corridor.

She stood there alone, with the silver I heavy on her chest, and discovered that she was smiling. She stopped immediately.

✦ ✦ ✦

She took a great many points that winter. She had to. Vivienne and Greta were always there, and Pansy, and the other Squad members, and Umbridge reviewed their reports every Friday.

She took points from Hufflepuffs for running. She took points from Ravenclaws for talking in the library. She took points from Gryffindors for every tiny thing, a crooked tie, a loud laugh, a book carried the wrong way up, because everyone was watching to see if she'd go easy on them.

From Harry Potter, she only ever took one point at a time.

For the hair. For the glasses, which were crooked. For the tie, which was always crooked. For the ink on his nose. For looking at her.

"That's not a rule," Harry said, the fourth time.

"It is now," said Draconia. "I'm on the Inquisitorial Squad. I can make rules."

"You can't..."

"One point from Gryffindor," she said, "for arguing with a member of the Inquisitorial Squad." She glanced down at the Charms homework sticking out of his bag, covered in wand diagrams that were far too advanced for anything they'd done in class. Shield Charms. Stunners. Disarming. "And what's all this? Since when are you top of the class, Potter? Running a little school with Longbottom now, are we?"

Harry shoved the parchment deeper into his bag. His ears had gone red. He couldn't lie. He never could.

"Just homework," he said.

"Of course it is," said Draconia sweetly. And walked away.

She never reported it. Not a single word to Umbridge. Not once.

She just wanted him to know that she knew.

✦ ✦ ✦

The news reached the dungeons on the last day of term before Christmas.

It came, as the worst news always did, through Millicent Bulstrode, at breakfast, through a mouthful of kippers.

"Did you hear? About Potter and Chang?"

Draconia went very still.

"Marietta Edgecombe told Padma Patil, who told Lavender Brown, who told the whole of Gryffindor," said Millicent happily. "Apparently they were alone, somewhere, last night, and there was mistletoe, and..."

"And?" said Pansy sharply, with a quick glance sideways.

"And he kissed her," said Millicent. "Or she kissed him. Nobody's sure which. Apparently she was crying the whole time, which is a bit odd, but..."

Draconia stood up.

"Draconia?" said Pansy.

"I've forgotten my Potions book," said Draconia, and walked out of the Great Hall.

She did not look at the Gryffindor table. She did not look at the Ravenclaw table. She walked out of the Great Hall with her back perfectly straight and her chin perfectly high, and then she walked up the marble staircase, and then up another staircase, and another, and another, all the way to the top of the Astronomy Tower, where it was freezing, and the wind cut straight through her robes, and there was nobody at all.

She stood at the parapet, and looked out over the frozen grounds, and did not cry.

She had never had any right to him. She knew that. She'd walked past him in corridors for six weeks last spring. She'd told him on the train that he'd picked the losing side. She'd joined Umbridge's squad. What had she expected? That he'd wait? That he'd sit in the Owlery every evening at eight o'clock, hoping, while she took points off him for his hair?

Cho Chang had never done any of those things.

Cho Chang had just been kind to him.

The wind was so cold that her eyes were streaming. That was all it was. The wind.

Footsteps on the stone stairs, behind her. She didn't turn round.

"You forgot your Potions book," said Pansy, breathless from the climb. "Apparently it's all the way up here."

Draconia didn't answer.

Pansy came and stood beside her at the parapet. She didn't say anything else. She didn't ask. She just unwound her own scarf, a thick green-and-silver one, and wrapped it twice around Draconia's neck, and stood there with her, in the freezing wind, for a very long time.

✦ ✦ ✦

Christmas at the Manor was grand and cold and very quiet.

On Christmas Eve, her father called her into his study again. He was in a good mood. He had heard about the Inquisitorial Squad from Dolores Umbridge herself, and he was pleased. He poured her a small glass of elf-made wine and toasted her, and she drank it, and it tasted like nothing.

"You have done well, Draconia," her father said. "Very well indeed. Your reports on the Potter boy have been noticed."

She had never sent any reports on the Potter boy. She had sent Umbridge a list of Hufflepuffs who ran in corridors. But she didn't say so.

"Noticed by whom, Father?"

Her father smiled.

"By someone," he said softly, "whose notice matters a great deal more than the Ministry's."

The wine turned to ice in her stomach.

"He asked about you, you know," her father went on, turning his glass. "By name. He was interested to hear that the Malfoy line had produced such a fine young heiress." He looked at her over the rim of his glass. "Such pure blood. Such an old name. Such a suitable age."

Draconia set her glass down on his desk, very carefully, so that it didn't rattle.

"May I go to bed, Father?" she said. "I'm very tired."

"Of course," said her father, still smiling. "Sleep well."

She didn't sleep at all.

✦ ✦ ✦

On Valentine's Day, Pansy dragged her to Madam Puddifoot's.

"You need cheering up," Pansy said firmly. "And the scones are nice. And if we go early, we'll get the good table."

Draconia hated Madam Puddifoot's. It was small and steamy and far too hot, and everything in it was decorated with frills and bows, and golden cherubs fluttered from the ceiling throwing pink confetti over the heads of the customers. And every table was full of couples holding hands and gazing at each other.

She sat by the window with a pot of tea she didn't want, and Pansy chattered, and Draconia stared at the confetti, and then the bell over the door tinkled, and she looked up.

It was Harry.

With Cho Chang.

Draconia's teacup stopped halfway to her mouth.

They sat down at a table only three away from hers. Harry looked profoundly uncomfortable. He kept glancing round at all the couples holding hands, and at the cherubs, and then at his tea, as if he'd walked into the Chamber of Secrets by mistake. Cho looked nervous, and pretty, in a blue jumper, with her dark hair shining.

"Don't look," hissed Pansy. "Draconia. Don't look."

Draconia looked.

She couldn't help it. She watched the entire thing, over the rim of her teacup, and it was the most painful and most fascinating half hour of her life.

It went wrong almost immediately. Harry didn't know what to say. Cho kept looking at the couples around them. The couple at the next table began kissing enthusiastically, and Harry stared into his tea with his ears flaming. Then Cho started talking about Cedric. Then Harry said something about having to meet Hermione at noon. Then Cho's face went very tight and very still.

"Hermione?" said Cho, loudly enough that half the tea shop heard. "You asked me out, and then you arranged to meet Hermione Granger in the middle of it?"

"No... it's not like... she just asked me to..."

"Well, I won't keep you," said Cho, standing up, pink and furious and already crying. "Enjoy yourself with Hermione."

She snatched up her coat and swept out of the tea shop. The bell jangled behind her. Every couple in Madam Puddifoot's turned to stare at Harry, sitting alone at his table, with his mouth open and a golden cherub fluttering over his head, sprinkling him with pink confetti.

He put his head in his hands.

Then he lifted it. And looked round the tea shop. And saw her.

Draconia froze with her teacup halfway to her mouth.

For a long moment, they stared at each other across three tables and a great deal of pink confetti.

And then, because she didn't know what else to do, she raised her teacup to him. Very slightly. Like a toast.

Harry stared at her. And then his shoulders started to shake. And he put his hand over his mouth, and turned it into a cough, and failed, and he was laughing, helplessly, silently, in the middle of Madam Puddifoot's, with confetti in his hair.

Draconia put her cup down and looked out of the window, very hard, so that nobody could see her face.

"Draconia," breathed Pansy. "Are you smiling?"

"Certainly not," said Draconia. "Drink your tea."

✦ ✦ ✦

The raid happened in March.

Somebody talked. Draconia found out later that it had been Marietta Edgecombe, Cho's friend; she'd gone to Umbridge in secret and told her everything. That there was a group. That it was called Dumbledore's Army. That it met in a secret room on the seventh floor, behind a blank stretch of wall opposite a tapestry of trolls in tutus, and that it was meeting tonight.

Umbridge came down to the Slytherin common room herself, flushed and triumphant, and gathered her Squad.

"Quickly," she breathed. "And quietly. I want every one of them."

They ran up through the castle in the dark. Seven floors of stairs. Draconia's heart was pounding so hard she thought she might be sick. She was at the back of the group. She'd made sure of that.

Harry. Harry. Harry.

They reached the seventh floor. They were twenty yards from the blank wall when a door appeared in it, suddenly, out of nowhere, and burst open, and students poured out of it like water from a broken dam.

"RUN!"

People scattered in every direction. Hufflepuffs and Ravenclaws and Gryffindors, fleeing down the corridor, down stairways, through tapestries. Umbridge was shrieking. The Squad fanned out, wands drawn, grabbing at people.

Draconia stood at the corner of the corridor, where it turned toward the staircase.

And Harry came round it, running flat out, straight at her.

He saw her. He saw the silver I on her chest. He saw her wand in her hand. And he stopped, dead, three feet away, and his face went white.

Behind him, she could hear Pansy coming, shouting. Twenty feet away. Ten.

"Draconia! He's there! Get him!"

Draconia looked at Harry. He looked back at her. His chest was heaving. He didn't raise his wand. He just stood there, waiting to see what she would do.

She raised hers.

"Tarantallegra," said Draconia clearly.

The spell shot past Harry's ear and hit Pansy square in the knees.

Pansy's legs went wild. They jerked and kicked and flailed into a frantic, uncontrollable jig, and she staggered sideways into a suit of armor with a tremendous crash, and went down in a heap of clanking metal, legs still dancing.

"Oops," said Draconia.

She didn't look at Harry. She looked at Pansy, on the floor, and said, loudly: "Pansy! Oh, Pansy, I'm so sorry, I was aiming at Potter, he ducked..."

When she looked up again, the corridor was empty.

He'd gone.

✦ ✦ ✦

It didn't matter in the end.

Umbridge had Marietta. And Marietta had a list. A sheet of parchment, pinned to the wall of the secret room, with every single member's name signed on it. And at the top, in large letters, DUMBLEDORE'S ARMY.

Harry's name was the first on the list.

Draconia's wasn't on it at all. She'd known it wouldn't be. She read the list anyway, in Umbridge's pink office, standing at the back of the Squad while Umbridge crowed over it. She read every single name, looking for her own, somehow, stupidly, as if it might have appeared there by magic.

It hadn't.

Cho's had.

Then Dumbledore walked into the office, and read the list, and said calmly that of course it was called Dumbledore's Army, because it was his army, and he had formed it himself. And when the Minister for Magic tried to arrest him, he vanished in a burst of flame, and was gone, and Umbridge was made headmistress of Hogwarts by the end of the week.

Harry, who had done everything, was punished for nothing.

Draconia, who had done nothing, was given fifty points for Slytherin for her work on the Inquisitorial Squad, which she accepted with her father's smile, and didn't feel at all.

✦ ✦ ✦

Pansy's legs stopped dancing about an hour after the raid, once Madam Pomfrey had found the countercurse. She came back to the dormitory limping, with a large purple bruise on her hip where she'd hit the suit of armor, and sat down on the end of Draconia's bed without being asked.

Draconia was pretending to read. She kept pretending.

"You weren't aiming at Potter," said Pansy.

Draconia turned a page.

"You're the best duellist in our year," Pansy went on quietly. "You've never missed a spell in your life. Not in class, not at the Dueling Club, not ever. And he was standing still. Three feet in front of you. He didn't even have his wand up." A pause. "You weren't aiming at him."

The dormitory was very quiet. Millicent was asleep. The lake moved green and black against the windows.

Draconia closed her book.

"No," she said. "I wasn't."

She waited for Pansy to be angry. To shout. To storm out and tell Vivienne, who would tell her father, who would tell Draconia's father, and then...

Pansy didn't do any of those things.

She sat there on the end of the bed, rubbing her bruised hip, and looked at Draconia for a long time with an odd, tired expression on her face.

"You know I've always known," Pansy said at last. "Since first year. Since you came back from the train with your hand shaking and wouldn't say why." She shrugged. "I'm not stupid, Draconia. I just pretend to be. It's easier."

"Pansy..."

"I'm not going to tell anyone." Pansy stood up, wincing. "But next time you want to jinx somebody out of your way, warn me first. I think I cracked a rib on that suit of armor."

She limped back to her own bed and drew the curtains.

Draconia sat in the dark for a long moment.

"Pansy," she said.

"What."

"Thank you."

There was a long silence from behind Pansy's curtains.

"It was a very good Dancing Feet Jinx," said Pansy's voice at last, grudgingly. "I'll give you that."

✦ ✦ ✦

The next morning, there was a note in her Transfiguration book.

It was tucked between the same two pages as all the others. The Prophet photograph. The note that said Found this on the path. The piece of parchment she'd pieced back together at midnight in fourth year. It was untidy, cramped, and slanted, and it said only one word.

Why?

She looked at it for a long time, sitting in the back row of the Transfiguration classroom, while McGonagall explained Vanishing Spells.

Then she dipped her quill, and wrote underneath it, in her neatest handwriting:

Manners, Potter.

And, smaller, underneath that:

You said you couldn't risk them. Well. Neither could I.

She left the book on the desk at the end of the lesson, open to that page, and walked out without looking back. When she went back for it at lunch, the note was gone.

In its place, there was a single new line in the same untidy handwriting.

One point to Slytherin. For manners.

Draconia put her face down on the Transfiguration desk, in the empty classroom, and laughed until she couldn't breathe.

✦ ✦ ✦

Chapter Thirteen: The Night Her Father Didn't Come Home

The Weasley twins left Hogwarts on a Thursday afternoon, and they did it on broomsticks, and they did it magnificently.

Draconia was on the fifth-floor corridor with the rest of the Inquisitorial Squad when it began. There was a bang, and a whoosh, and the whole east wing filled with smoke, and then fireworks started pouring out of a classroom door: dragons made of green and gold sparks, roaring down the corridors; Catherine wheels spinning like saws; rockets with long silver tails that shot along the ceiling and exploded into stars; sparklers that wrote rude words in the air all by themselves.

Umbridge came running, shrieking, with her wand out. She Stunned a Catherine wheel. It exploded into ten more Catherine wheels.

Draconia stood against the wall, with her silver I on her chest, and watched a fireworks dragon chase Professor Umbridge down the whole length of the corridor, and felt something rise in her chest that she hadn't felt in months.

She was trying very hard not to laugh.

She almost managed it. Right up until the moment the Weasley twins came streaking past on their brooms, low over the heads of the crowd, whooping, trailing sparks. And Fred Weasley, or possibly George, looked down, and saw her pressed against the wall, and saw the badge on her chest, and grinned.

"Nice badge, Malfoy!" he shouted. "Liked the last one better! Potter's hair stinks!"

And then they were gone, out through the great front doors, soaring up into the sunset over the lake, while the whole school cheered.

Draconia put her hand over her mouth and shook with silent laughter until tears came out of her eyes, and Pansy had to pretend to be helping her because she was overcome by smoke.

✦ ✦ ✦

The O.W.L. exams began the following week.

The Great Hall was filled with rows of single desks, and for two weeks, the fifth years sat in them, writing until their hands ached. Draconia did well. She knew she would. She'd been studying since Christmas, mostly so that she had something to do in the evenings that wasn't the Owlery.

The last exam was History of Magic, on a hot, heavy afternoon at the very end of term. She was halfway through a question about goblin rebellions when she heard it.

A cry. Behind her.

She turned round in her seat. Everyone did.

Harry was on the floor.

He'd fallen out of his chair. He was clutching his forehead, his scar, with both hands, and his face was twisted with something that looked like agony, and he was shouting. Not words. Just a name. Over and over.

"Sirius! No... Sirius..."

Two examiners rushed over and lifted him up and half carried him out of the Great Hall, and he was still shouting, still clutching his head, as the doors closed behind him.

Draconia sat frozen at her desk, her quill dripping ink onto her parchment, staring at the doors.

She didn't know who Sirius was. The only Sirius she'd ever heard of was Sirius Black, the escaped murderer, her mother's own cousin, who had vanished two years ago and never been caught.

She didn't finish the exam. She couldn't remember afterward what she'd written.

✦ ✦ ✦

An hour later, she was standing in Umbridge's office, holding Harry Potter's arm.

It had happened so fast. Umbridge had come down to the Slytherin common room, pink-faced and furious, calling for her Squad. Potter has broken into my office. He's trying to use my fireplace. Come at once. And they'd run, all of them, up to the pink office. And there he was, on his knees in front of the fireplace with his head in the green flames, trying to talk to someone through the Floo Network. And there were Hermione and Ron and Ginny Weasley and Neville Longbottom and Luna Lovegood, all trying to keep watch, and all caught.

Umbridge had seized Harry by the hair and dragged him out of the fire. And then she'd pointed at Draconia and said: "You. Hold him."

So Draconia was holding him.

She had her hand clamped on his upper arm. She could feel him shaking. Not with fear. With something worse. Something frantic and desperate and wild, like an animal caught in a trap.

"Who were you talking to?" Umbridge was demanding. "Who, Potter? Dumbledore? Hagrid?"

"Nobody," said Harry.

His arm was trembling under her hand. Draconia leaned very slightly closer, so that only he could hear.

"What happened?" she breathed. "In the exam. Who's Sirius?"

He didn't answer. But he turned his head, just slightly, and looked at her, and his eyes were so full of terror that she felt it go into her like cold water.

"Very well," said Umbridge. Her voice had gone soft and sweet, the voice she used when she was at her most dangerous. "You leave me no choice, Potter." She raised her wand. "The Cruciatus Curse ought to loosen your tongue."

The whole office went dead silent.

"Professor," said Draconia.

She hadn't decided to speak. It just came out.

Umbridge's head turned. Her bulging eyes fixed on Draconia.

"That's an Unforgivable Curse," Draconia said. Her voice was steady. She didn't know how. "It's illegal. You'd get a life sentence in Azkaban for using it on a student. My father..." She swallowed. "My father would not approve."

For a long moment, Umbridge simply looked at her.

Then she smiled. It was the worst smile Draconia had ever seen.

"How very touching, Miss Malfoy," she said softly. "But what the Minister does not know, the Minister cannot mind. Can he?" She turned back to Harry. "Stand aside, dear."

Draconia didn't stand aside. She didn't let go of his arm.

And then Hermione burst into tears.

"Tell her, Harry!" she sobbed. "Just tell her! About the... the weapon!"

Everything stopped. Umbridge lowered her wand, slowly, her eyes narrowing with greedy interest. And Hermione, sobbing and hiccuping, started telling her a long, stammering story about a secret weapon that Dumbledore had hidden in the Forbidden Forest, and how she and Harry could take her to it, right now, if she liked...

Draconia had watched Hermione Granger for five years. She had seen her furious, and frightened, and triumphant, and slap-happy. She had never, once, seen her cry in front of an enemy.

She's lying, Draconia realized. She's lying, and Umbridge is going to believe her.

Umbridge did.

"Very well," she said, breathless with excitement. "You two will show me. Now. The rest of you..." She turned to the Squad. "Keep these ones here. Don't let them out of your sight."

And she marched Harry and Hermione out of the office, down toward the forest, and Harry looked back at Draconia over his shoulder as he went, just once.

She let go of his arm.

✦ ✦ ✦

There were five of them left in the office, and six of the Squad.

Vivienne had Ron Weasley in an armlock. Greta was holding Neville. Millicent had Ginny Weasley, and a sixth-year boy called Warrington had Luna Lovegood, who didn't appear to have noticed that she'd been captured and was humming quietly to herself. Pansy was standing by the door.

And Draconia was standing by the desk.

Where Umbridge had put all their confiscated wands.

She looked at them. Six wands, in a little pile, on the pink tablecloth. She looked at Ron's red, furious face. At Ginny, who was glaring at Millicent as if she'd like to set her on fire. At Neville, who looked terrified and determined in equal measure.

At Pansy, by the door. Who was looking straight back at her.

Pansy raised one eyebrow. Very slightly.

Draconia picked up Ginny Weasley's wand.

"Weasley," she said sharply. Ginny's head snapped round. "Catch."

She threw it.

Ginny caught it one-handed, without even looking surprised, spun it once in her fingers, pointed it straight past her own shoulder at Millicent's face, and said "Bat-Bogey!" And Millicent's nose exploded in a cloud of huge, flapping, bat-winged bogeys, and she screamed and let go.

After that, everything happened very fast.

Draconia threw the other wands, one after another, across the office. Ron's. Neville's. Luna's. Vivienne was shouting; Warrington was diving for Luna; spells were flying in every direction; somebody knocked into the wall, and all Umbridge's kitten plates came crashing down, mewing in horror. And through all of it, Pansy stood by the door with her arms folded, and did absolutely nothing at all, and when Vivienne screamed at her to help, Pansy said, calmly, "I've hurt my hip."

It was over in thirty seconds.

Vivienne, Greta, Millicent and Warrington were lying on the pink carpet, Stunned or jinxed or buried in bogeys. Ron and Ginny and Neville and Luna were standing in the middle of the wreckage, panting, wands raised.

Every single wand was pointed at Draconia.

"Why?" said Ron Weasley. His voice was hoarse. "Why'd you do that?"

Draconia lifted her chin.

"Harry's in trouble," she said. "Isn't he? Something happened in that exam. Something to do with someone called Sirius. And you're going to go and help him, whatever it is, because that's what you do." She pointed at the door. "So go. Now. Before I change my mind."

They looked at each other.

"Thanks," said Ginny, abruptly.

Then they were gone, running, out of the office and down the corridor, toward the forest and Harry and whatever was waiting there.

Draconia stood in the middle of the wrecked pink office, among the smashed kitten plates, and the Stunned bodies of her own house, and she started shaking and couldn't stop.

Pansy came over and stood beside her.

"We'll say they overpowered us," said Pansy. "All of us. Six against five. Very embarrassing. Terrible for our house pride." She looked down at Vivienne, lying Stunned on the carpet with her mouth open. "She won't remember any different. None of them will. They were all facing the other way."

Draconia looked at her.

"You didn't help," she said. "You just stood there."

"I told you," said Pansy. "I've hurt my hip."

✦ ✦ ✦

Harry didn't come back that night.

None of them did. Not Harry, or Hermione, or Ron, or Ginny, or Neville, or Luna. Umbridge came back, though, late, carried up from the forest by Dumbledore, of all people, who had somehow returned: she was mute and shaking and staring at nothing, with twigs in her hair, and she never taught at Hogwarts again.

Draconia waited in the Owlery all night.

She sat on the cold stone floor under the window, where she had sat so many times before, and watched the stars go round, and didn't sleep. She didn't know where Harry was. She didn't know what was happening. She only knew that something terrible was, and that she wasn't there.

At dawn, the owls came back from the night's hunting. At breakfast, the Daily Prophet came.

She read it standing up in the entrance hall. She didn't even make it to the Slytherin table.

HE WHO MUST NOT BE NAMED RETURNS

The Minister for Magic confirmed last night that He Who Must Not Be Named has returned to this country and is active once more. A battle took place late last night inside the Ministry of Magic itself, in which a number of Death Eaters were apprehended. Among those arrested...

She read the list of names.

She read it three times.

Crabbe. Goyle. Nott. Avery. Macnair.

Lucius Malfoy.

The newspaper slipped out of her hands. It fell open on the flagstones at her feet, and her father's photograph looked up at her from the front page, his face pale and furious, his long hair wild, his hands bound, being led away between two Aurors.

He was struggling in the photograph. He kept struggling, over and over, the way people in wizard photographs did.

Around her, the entrance hall was filling with people, and all of them were reading the same newspaper, and all of them were turning, one by one, to look at her.

Draconia picked the Prophet up off the floor. She folded it very small, the way she always folded things. She put it inside her Transfiguration book, with all the others.

Then she walked into the Great Hall, and sat down at the Slytherin table, and ate her breakfast. Every single bite. With her back perfectly straight and her chin perfectly high and three hundred people watching her do it.

It was the hardest thing she had ever done in her life.

✦ ✦ ✦

She went to the hospital wing that afternoon.

She told herself she was going for a Headache Draught. She had a headache; that part was true. She walked up four flights of stairs with her book bag over her shoulder and her face perfectly composed, and she stopped outside the double doors, and she looked through the little window in the left one.

They were all in there. Ron, in the bed nearest the door, with long red weals wrapped round his arms like rope burns. Neville, with a bandaged nose. Luna, sitting up and reading a magazine upside down. And at the far end, Hermione Granger, lying very still and very white, with a jug of something purple on the table beside her and Madam Pomfrey bending over her, frowning.

Draconia's hand was on the door handle. She didn't turn it.

What was she going to say? Hello, I'm the daughter of the man who did this to you. I've brought grapes.

"He's not in there."

She spun round.

Ginny Weasley was standing on the landing behind her, with one arm in a sling and a bottle of pumpkin juice in her other hand. She had a bruise on her cheekbone the colour of a plum, and she was looking at Draconia with no expression at all.

"I wasn't looking for anyone," said Draconia.

"Course you weren't." Ginny came up the last step. She looked through the window too, at her brother, at Hermione. Her mouth went tight. "Hermione got hit with something in the chest. Pomfrey says she'll be fine. She'll need about ten potions a day for a fortnight, but she'll be fine." She paused. "Ron got attacked by a brain. Don't ask."

Draconia didn't ask.

"Your dad was there," said Ginny.

"I know."

"He was the one who kept shouting at the others. To be careful. To get the prophecy first and not to hurt Harry until they had it." Ginny looked at her sideways. "I thought you'd want to know he wasn't the one who... who did the worst of it. It was your aunt."

Draconia stared at her.

Ginny Weasley, whom her father had once tricked with a cursed diary. Ginny Weasley, whom Draconia had called a blood traitor to her face more times than she could count. Standing on a landing with her arm in a sling, telling her something that would make it hurt a little bit less.

"Why are you telling me that?" said Draconia.

Ginny shrugged with her good shoulder.

"You threw me my wand," she said. She pushed the hospital wing door open with her hip. "He's down by the lake. He's been there since lunch. Nobody's managed to get a word out of him." She stopped, halfway through the door, and looked back. "Maybe you'll have better luck."

The door swung shut behind her.

✦ ✦ ✦

She found him by the lake.

He was sitting on the same flat rock where she'd sat after the Quidditch final in third year, the day he'd told her he owed her four. He was staring out at the water. He hadn't eaten anything all day; she knew, because she'd watched the Gryffindor table at every single meal, and he hadn't been there.

He didn't turn round when she came down the bank. He must have heard her. He didn't move.

She sat down on the grass beside the rock, a little below him, and looked out at the water too.

For a long time, neither of them said anything.

"Sirius Black," she said at last, very quietly. "Was it him? The Sirius you were shouting for?"

Harry didn't answer for a long moment.

"He was my godfather," he said.

She turned and looked up at him.

"He didn't do it. Any of it. Everything they said he did. He was innocent. He was my dad's best friend, and he was my godfather, and he was the only family I had." Harry's voice was flat and hollow, the voice of someone who had cried until there was nothing left. "And he died last night. At the Ministry. Trying to help me. Because I was stupid enough to believe a dream."

Draconia couldn't breathe.

He was my mother's cousin, she thought. He was my family too. I never met him. I never even knew.

"Your dad was there," said Harry. "I suppose you know that."

"Yes."

"He was the one in charge. He tried to take something from me. A prophecy. He said..." Harry stopped. His hands were clenched on his knees. "He said a lot of things."

The lake lapped quietly at the stones.

Draconia had been Draconia Malfoy for fifteen years, and in all of that time, she had never, once, said sorry to anyone. Not to the house-elves. Not to Hagrid. Not to Hermione, on the bank of the lake, when she'd almost managed it. A Malfoy never apologizes, her father used to say. To apologize is to admit that you were wrong, and a Malfoy is never wrong.

Her father was in Azkaban.

"I'm sorry," said Draconia.

Harry turned his head and looked at her.

"I'm sorry," she said again. Her voice cracked right down the middle. "About Sirius. About my father. About all of it. I'm sorry, Harry."

It was the first time she'd ever said his name.

Not Potter. Not Four Eyes. Just Harry, out loud, in the evening air by the lake, and it came out of her as easily as if she'd been saying it all her life.

Harry stared at her. Something in his face broke open.

"It's not your fault," he said hoarsely. "What your dad did. It's not your fault."

"I know," said Draconia. "I'm still sorry."

He came down off the rock. He sat down on the grass beside her, close, so close their shoulders were touching. And then, very slowly, as if he wasn't sure he was allowed, he put his head down on her shoulder.

She put her arm around him.

They sat there together by the lake, as the sun went down behind the mountains and the water turned from gold to grey to black, and neither of them said anything else at all.

They didn't need to.

✦ ✦ ✦

Her mother met her at King's Cross.

Narcissa was alone. She was wearing black, and she looked ten years older than she had at Christmas, and when Draconia walked through the barrier, she took her daughter's face in both of her cold hands and looked at her for a long time without speaking.

"He'll be home soon," her mother said at last. "The Dark Lord will see to it. He takes care of his own."

Draconia didn't say anything.

Behind her, across the platform, she knew that Harry was standing with the Weasleys. She didn't look back this time. She didn't dare, with her mother's hands on her face.

But she felt the green silk ribbon on his wrist, the whole way out of the station, like a warm spot in the sun.

✦ ✦ ✦

The summer was very quiet at Malfoy Manor, and then, one night in July, it wasn't.

She was woken by a sound she had never heard before. Not the crack of Apparition on the gravel. Something else. Something like a long, low hiss, like the breath of an enormous snake, rolling through every room in the house at once.

She went to the top of the stairs in her nightdress.

The doors to the great dining room were open, and every candle in it was lit, and the long table was full of people in black cloaks. Her mother was sitting near the end, very pale and very still. Her aunt Bellatrix, whom she'd only ever seen in newspaper photographs, was sitting close to the head of the table, gazing at it with an expression of shining devotion.

And in her father's chair, at the head of her father's table, sat a thin, tall figure with skin as white as bone, and a face like a snake's, and eyes as red as blood.

An enormous serpent was coiled around the legs of his chair.

Draconia stood frozen at the top of the stairs.

And the red eyes lifted, slowly, and looked straight up through the open doors, and found her.

"Ah," said Lord Voldemort softly. His voice was high and cold and filled the whole house. "So this is Lucius's daughter."

Every head at the table turned to look at her.

"Come down, child," said the Dark Lord. "Come here, where I can see you." He smiled. It was a lipless, terrible smile. "I have heard so much about you."
